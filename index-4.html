A1.1 — Subject-Verb Agreement

▼
Meaning

This rule simply says that a singular subject needs a singular verb, and a plural subject needs a plural verb. It sounds easy at first, but the exam likes to hide the real subject behind tricky words, extra phrases, or unusual sentence order — so the real skill here is learning to find the true subject before choosing your verb. Many students get this wrong not because they don't know the rule, but because they match the verb to the wrong word in the sentence.

Examples
  • "Mathematics is my hardest subject." (looks plural because of the "s," but it names one single subject)
  • "Neither the coach nor the players were happy." (the verb matches the closer subject, "players")
  • "The basket of apples is on the table." (the subject is "basket," not "apples" — "of apples" is just extra description)
  • "There are three cats in the yard." (the real subject, "cats," comes after the verb, not before)
  • "Everyone in the class is required to attend." (even though "class" sounds like a group, "everyone" is the true subject, and it's always singular)
Application

When you read a sentence, find the true subject first — cover up or ignore any describing phrases sitting between the subject and the verb, since they never affect which verb form you use. Watch out for these common traps:

  • Words like mathematics, physics, news, measles look plural because they end in "s," but they act as a single, singular subject.
  • With "or/nor," the verb always agrees with whichever subject sits closest to it, not the first one mentioned.
  • Indefinite pronouns like each, everyone, either, neither, somebody are always treated as singular, even when they seem to refer to a group of people.
  • Amounts of money, time, or distance are usually treated as one single unit rather than counted individually — "Ten dollars is enough," not "are enough," because we're thinking of it as one amount.
  • In sentences that start with "there is/are" or "here is/are," the subject actually comes after the verb, so you need to find it before deciding which verb form to use.
Explanation

The confusion in this topic usually comes from something sitting between the subject and the verb, or from a sentence being flipped around so the subject appears in an unusual position. The fix is always the same, no matter how tricky the sentence looks: cross out the extra describing words, locate the one true noun that is actually doing the action, and match your verb to that noun alone. Once you build the habit of finding the "real" subject first, this becomes one of the easiest topics to get consistently right, since the rule itself never changes — only the disguise around it does.

1. "The committee ___ meeting every Friday."
a) is b) are
a) is — committee is one unit here.
2. "Neither the students nor the teacher ___ prepared for the fire drill."
a) was b) were
a) was — agrees with "teacher" (closer subject).
3. "The box of chocolates ___ already empty."
a) is b) are
a) is — subject is "box," not "chocolates."
4. "Everyone in the room ___ excited about the field trip."
a) is b) are
a) is — "everyone" is always singular.
5. "There ___ several reasons why the project failed."
a) is b) are
b) are — real subject is "reasons" (plural), found after "there."

A1.2 — Verb Tense, Aspect, and Consistency

▼
Meaning

Tense tells you when something happened — past, present, or future. Aspect tells you how the action unfolds — whether it's finished, still going on, or connected to another moment in time. This topic checks whether you can place events correctly along a timeline and keep that timeline consistent throughout a sentence or paragraph, without accidentally jumping backward or forward for no reason.

Examples
  • "I have finished my project." (present perfect — the action is finished, but it still matters right now, and no exact time is mentioned)
  • "She had left before I arrived." (past perfect — this shows which of two past events happened first)
  • "They are studying right now." (present progressive — an action that is happening at this exact moment)
  • "By next year, I will have graduated." (future perfect — an action that will be complete before a specific point in the future)
  • "If I were taller, I would join the team." (subjunctive mood — describes a wish or a situation that isn't actually true)
Application

Ask yourself: did this happen before, during, or after another action in the sentence? If two things happened in the past but one happened clearly before the other, use the past perfect ("had + participle") for the earlier event, and simple past for the later one. If a sentence describes a wish, a guess, or a hypothetical situation that isn't real, use "were" instead of "was," even with subjects like "I" or "he/she" — this is called the subjunctive mood, and it's a common trap on the exam. Also pay close attention to irregular verbs (like go/went/gone or write/wrote/written), since their past tense and past participle forms don't follow the usual "-ed" pattern and are easy to mix up.

Explanation

Most tense errors happen when a sentence starts off in one tense and then quietly switches to another without a good reason — for example, starting a story in the past tense and then suddenly describing an action in the present tense. To avoid this, read the whole sentence (or even the whole paragraph) as one single timeline in your head, and make sure every verb fits naturally into that same timeline unless the meaning genuinely requires a shift, like moving from something that already happened to something that is still ongoing. With practice, this becomes a matter of "listening" to whether the sentence sounds consistent, rather than memorizing complicated rules.

1. "By the time the ambulance arrived, the patient ___ already passed away."
a) has b) had
b) had — past perfect; happened before another past event.
2. Spot the error: "Yesterday, she goes to the market and bought fish."
"goes" should be "went" — both actions are in the past, need consistency.
3. "If I ___ you, I would apologize first."
a) was b) were
b) were — subjunctive for hypothetical.
4. Choose the correct past participle: "The window was ___ during the storm."
a) broke b) broken
b) broken — past participle used with "was" (passive voice).
5. "The teacher requested that every student ___ their assignment on time."
a) submits b) submit
b) submit — subjunctive after a formal request/demand.

A1.3 — Pronouns

▼
Meaning

A pronoun is a word that stands in for a noun so we don't have to keep repeating the same name (like using "he" instead of saying "John" every single time). This topic checks two things: first, whether you can clearly match a pronoun to the exact noun it's replacing, and second, whether you're using the correct form of that pronoun depending on its job in the sentence — whether it's doing the action or receiving it.

Examples
  • "Each student must submit his/her essay." (the pronoun matches the singular noun "each student")
  • "Whom did you call?" (object form, since "whom" is receiving the action of calling, not doing it)
  • "Please send the report to John or me." (object pronoun — not "myself," since there's no reason to use a reflexive pronoun here)
  • "My car, which is red, needs repair." (this gives extra, non-essential information about the car)
  • "The book that I borrowed is due tomorrow." (this identifies specifically which book — essential information)
Application

First, find the noun the pronoun is replacing (called its antecedent), and make sure the two match in number — singular pronoun for a singular noun, plural for plural. Second, check whether the pronoun is doing the action in the sentence (subject form: I, he, she, they, who) or receiving the action (object form: me, him, her, them, whom). A simple trick for "who" vs. "whom": if you could answer the question with "he" or "she," use "who"; if you'd answer with "him" or "her," use "whom." Only use reflexive pronouns (myself, himself, themselves) when the subject and the object of the sentence are literally the same person — never just to sound more polite or formal.

Explanation

Confusion in this topic happens most often when a pronoun could refer to more than one noun in the sentence, leaving the reader unsure who or what is actually being talked about — this is called a vague or ambiguous reference, and it's a common error type tested on the exam. Another frequent mistake is using "myself" simply because it sounds more formal or polite, even though it's grammatically incorrect unless the subject and object are the same person. The best habit to build is tracing every pronoun back to one clear, specific noun before deciding which form fits — if you can't clearly identify what the pronoun refers to, the sentence likely needs to be rewritten.

1. "Each of the boys forgot ___ jacket at school."
a) his b) their
a) his — "each" is singular (traditional grammar rule).
2. "___ is going to the party tonight?"
a) Who b) Whom
a) Who — subject of the sentence.
3. Spot the error: "The manager gave the assignment to Sarah and myself."
"myself" should be "me" — reflexive pronoun misused; should be object pronoun.
4. "This backpack is ___ than mine."
a) hers b) her
a) hers — possessive pronoun (stands alone, no noun after it).
5. Identify the vague pronoun reference: "When the teacher spoke to the principal, she seemed upset." What's unclear?
It's unclear whether "she" refers to the teacher or the principal — needs to be rewritten with a specific name.

A1.4 — Modifiers & Word Order

▼
Meaning

A modifier is a word or phrase that describes or gives more information about something else in the sentence — an adjective describing a noun, or an adverb describing a verb. This topic tests whether the modifier is placed close enough to the exact word it's meant to describe, because if it's too far away, the sentence can accidentally end up saying something completely different from what you intended.

Examples
  • Wrong: "She almost drove her kids to school every day." (this sounds like she almost did the whole activity of driving, which isn't what's meant)
  • Right: "She drove her kids to school almost every day." (now "almost" correctly describes "every day," meaning she did it on most days but not all)
  • Wrong: "Walking to school, the rain started pouring." (this literally suggests the rain was the one walking, which makes no sense)
  • Right: "Walking to school, I got caught in the rain." (now it's clear that "I" is the one walking)
  • "She sings well." (adverb, describing the verb "sings" — how she sings) vs. "She is a good singer." (adjective, describing the noun "singer" — what kind of singer she is)
Application

When you read a sentence, check exactly which word the modifier is sitting closest to — is that really the word it should be describing, or did it accidentally attach itself to the wrong one? If a sentence starts with a describing phrase (like "Walking to school..." or "After finishing the race..."), the very next word after the comma should be the person or thing actually doing that action; if it isn't, the modifier is "dangling" with nothing to attach to. Also double-check whether a word is describing an action (a verb) — which needs the adverb form (usually ending in "-ly") — or describing a person or thing (a noun) — which needs the adjective form instead.

Explanation

These errors happen because English allows a fair amount of freedom in how you arrange words, which means a modifier can accidentally end up attached to the wrong part of the sentence without the writer even noticing. The fix is always the same: physically move the modifier so it sits directly next to the exact word or person it's meant to describe, or, if the sentence is missing a clear "doer" altogether, add one in. Reading a sentence slowly and literally — asking "does this actually make sense as written?" — is often enough to catch these errors, since misplaced and dangling modifiers tend to create sentences that sound a little strange or even silly once you notice them.

1. Fix the misplaced modifier: "He served pizza to the guests on paper plates."
"He served pizza on paper plates to the guests." (the plates, not the guests, are on paper)
2. Fix the dangling modifier: "After finishing the race, a medal was given to her."
"After finishing the race, she was given a medal." (need a clear doer)
3. "He speaks ___ for a foreigner."
a) good b) well
b) well — describes the verb "speaks," needs an adverb.
4. Identify the error: "I don't want no dessert tonight."
Double negative — should be "I don't want any dessert tonight."
5. Explain the meaning difference: "She only smiled at him" vs "Only she smiled at him."
First = smiling was the only thing she did (didn't speak, wave, etc.); Second = she was the only person who smiled (no one else did).

A1.5 — Parallelism & Comparisons

▼
Meaning

Parallelism means keeping the same grammatical form for every item in a list or a pair of ideas, so the sentence flows smoothly and evenly instead of feeling awkward or lopsided. Comparisons, meanwhile, need to compare things that are actually the same type of thing — not accidentally compare a thing to a completely unrelated category, which is a subtle error that's easy to miss on a first read.

Examples
  • Wrong: "She likes reading, to write, and painting." → Right: "She likes reading, writing, and painting." (all three items now end in "-ing," matching in form)
  • Wrong: "The price of milk is higher than soda." → Right: "The price of milk is higher than that of soda." (this compares price to price, not price to an actual drink)
  • "I have less homework tonight." (uncountable — you can't count "homework" one by one) vs. "I have fewer assignments tonight." (countable — you can count assignments individually: one, two, three)
  • "She is taller than her brother." (comparative form, used when comparing exactly two people) vs. "She is the tallest in her family." (superlative form, used when comparing three or more people)
  • "Not only did he apologize, but he also offered to help." (parallel verb forms after "not only... but also")
Application

When you see a list joined by commas or the word "and," check that every single item in that list has the same grammatical shape — either all verbs in "-ing" form, all plain nouns, or all simple verbs, but not a mix of different forms. For comparison sentences, read carefully and ask yourself: "Am I comparing two genuinely similar things, or did I accidentally compare a thing to a completely different kind of thing?" For "fewer" versus "less," a quick test is to ask if you could count the item one at a time — if yes, use "fewer"; if it's more like a general amount or substance, use "less."

Explanation

Parallelism errors usually happen when a writer starts a list using one grammatical form and then unconsciously drifts into a different one partway through, often without even realizing it while writing quickly. Comparison errors, on the other hand, usually happen when a sentence accidentally drops an important connecting word (like "that of" or "compared to"), causing the two sides of the comparison to no longer match up logically, even though the sentence might still "sound fine" on a quick read. The best way to catch both types of errors is to slow down and read the sentence item by item, or side by side for comparisons, rather than reading the whole thing quickly as one block.

1. Fix the parallelism error: "The recipe requires mixing the batter, to preheat the oven, and greasing the pan."
"...mixing the batter, preheating the oven, and greasing the pan."
2. "I have ___ homework tonight than I did yesterday."
a) fewer b) less
b) less — homework is uncountable.
3. Fix the illogical comparison: "The population of Manila is larger than Cebu."
"The population of Manila is larger than that of Cebu." (comparing population to population)
4. "Of the two answers, this one is ___."
a) better b) best
a) better — comparative, only 2 items being compared.
5. Fix: "He is both intelligent and a hard worker."
"He is both intelligent and hard-working." (parallel adjective forms) — or "He is both an intelligent person and a hard worker" (parallel nouns).

A1.6 — Sentence Structure & Common Errors

▼
Meaning

This topic checks whether you can tell a truly complete sentence apart from a broken one — either too short and unfinished (called a fragment) or two full sentences squeezed together incorrectly without the right connector (called a run-on). Being able to spot these quickly is one of the most practical skills for both grammar questions and for improving your own writing.

Examples
  • Fragment: "Because she was exhausted." (this leaves us hanging — we know why, but not what happened as a result)
  • Complete: "Because she was exhausted, she went to bed early." (now the sentence delivers a full, complete thought)
  • Run-on/comma splice: "I like pizza, I eat it every week." (two complete sentences joined by only a comma, which isn't strong enough on its own)
  • Fixed: "I like pizza. I eat it every week." or "I like pizza, and I eat it every week." or "I like pizza; I eat it every week."
  • Voice inconsistency: "He cooked dinner, and the dishes were washed by her." → Fixed: "He cooked dinner, and she washed the dishes." (both halves now use active voice)
Application

Read the sentence and ask yourself: does it have a clear subject, a verb, and a complete thought that could stand on its own? If a sentence starts with a word like "because," "although," "since," or "when," and nothing else follows to complete the thought, it's a fragment. If you notice two complete sentences joined by nothing but a comma, that's a comma splice — you'll need to fix it using a period, a semicolon, or a proper conjunction like "and," "but," or "so" instead. It also helps to check that the whole sentence stays in the same voice throughout — either both halves active (subject does the action) or both passive (subject receives the action), rather than mixing the two.

Explanation

Fragments happen because a dependent clause — one that starts with a word like "although" or "because" — can sound like it's expressing a complete idea, when in reality it's "leaning" on a main clause that never actually shows up in the sentence. Run-ons happen for the opposite reason: when we write quickly, we sometimes connect two genuinely complete ideas using just a comma, when the relationship between them actually needs a stronger connector to hold them together properly. Learning to tell the difference between a clause that can "stand on its own" (independent) and one that needs support from another clause (dependent) is really the key skill that solves both of these common problems at once.

1. Is this a fragment or a complete sentence? "Since the store was closed."
Fragment — dependent clause with no main clause attached.
2. Fix the run-on: "The exam was difficult many students struggled to finish."
"The exam was difficult; many students struggled to finish." (or use a period, or add "so")
3. Fix the faulty coordination: "She studied hard, and she failed the test."
"Although she studied hard, she failed the test." (contrast, not addition)
4. Fix the voice inconsistency: "The teacher graded the essays, and the feedback was given by her the next day."
"The teacher graded the essays, and she gave the feedback the next day."
5. Identify which is an independent clause: "Although he was tired" or "He was tired."
"He was tired" — it can stand alone; "Although he was tired" is dependent (needs a main clause).

Punctuation & Mechanics

A2 — Punctuation & Mechanics

▼
Meaning

This topic covers the small but powerful marks — commas, semicolons, colons, apostrophes, hyphens — that hold a sentence together and shape its meaning. Even though these marks are tiny, using the wrong one can genuinely confuse the reader or change what a sentence actually means, which is why each mark has one specific, consistent job that it's meant to do, and mixing them up is the most common mistake students make on this topic.

Examples
  • Comma: "After the game, we went home." (used after an introductory phrase, before the main part of the sentence begins)
  • Semicolon: "I have a test tomorrow; I need to study." (joins two closely related, complete sentences without needing a conjunction like "and")
  • Colon: "She packed three things: a book, a jacket, and snacks." (introduces a list, but only works after a sentence that could already stand complete on its own)
  • Apostrophe: "It's almost time for dinner, and the dog wagged its tail." (it's = it is/it has; its = possessive form, and it never takes an apostrophe)
  • Hyphen: "a well-known author" (hyphenated when the two describing words come directly before the noun) vs. "the author is well known" (no hyphen needed when the words come after the noun instead)
Application

Before using a semicolon or a colon, always check what comes right before it — is that part a complete sentence on its own? If yes, both marks can work there; if not, you likely need a comma instead. Before writing "it's," pause and ask yourself if you actually mean "it is" — if the answer is no, you almost certainly want "its" instead, with no apostrophe at all. When two describing words are working together directly in front of a noun (like "well-known author" or "fast-moving car"), connect them with a hyphen; but if those same words appear after the noun instead, the hyphen is dropped.

Explanation

Most punctuation mistakes happen because these marks get treated as if they're interchangeable, when in fact each one carries a slightly different "strength" or purpose — a comma is the weakest connector, a semicolon is stronger and joins two complete ideas, and a colon works almost like an announcement, introducing something that follows. The most reliable trick for mastering this topic is to always check what comes immediately before the punctuation mark: is it a complete thought, or not? That single question usually tells you exactly which mark actually belongs there, without needing to memorize a long list of separate rules.

1. Choose correctly: "___ almost time for dinner, and the dog wagged ___ tail."
"It's almost time for dinner, and the dog wagged its tail."
2. Fix the colon error: "My favorite subjects are: math, science, and English."
"My favorite subjects are math, science, and English." (no colon — "are" isn't a complete clause on its own before a list)
3. Choose the correct word: "The rainy weather will surely ___ our travel plans."
a) affect b) effect
a) affect — verb meaning "to influence."
4. Punctuate correctly: "I woke up early I had a big exam that day"
"I woke up early; I had a big exam that day." (or use a period, or "because")
5. Choose the correct hyphenation: "The (well known / well-known) singer performed live." vs. "The singer is (well known / well-known)."
"The well-known singer performed live." (before noun — hyphenated) / "The singer is well known." (after — no hyphen)

Diction, Style, and Sentence Improvement

A3 — Diction, Style, and Sentence Improvement

▼
Meaning

This topic is about writing clearly and directly — cutting out unnecessary words, choosing an appropriate tone for the situation, and picking the version of a sentence that expresses the same idea in the clearest, most effective way possible. These often show up as "which of the following sentences is the best revision?" type questions, which test not just correctness, but overall quality and clarity of writing.

Examples
  • Wordy: "due to the fact that" → Concise: "because" (says the exact same thing in far fewer words)
  • Informal: "The results were kinda surprising." → Formal: "The results were somewhat surprising." (more appropriate for academic or professional writing)
  • Vague: "The situation was bad." → Precise: "The economy entered a recession." (gives the reader an actual, specific picture instead of a vague feeling)
  • Passive: "The plan was approved by the committee." → Active: "The committee approved the plan." (more direct, and usually a few words shorter too)
  • Contrast transition: "The team trained hard every day; however, they still lost." (signals that the second idea goes against what you'd expect from the first)
Application

When revising a sentence, first scan for wordy or padded phrases that could be shortened without losing any of the original meaning — common culprits include "due to the fact that," "in order to," and "at this point in time." Next, check the overall tone: does it match a formal or academic setting, or does it accidentally slip into casual, spoken-style language? It's also worth checking whether a sentence would sound clearer written in active voice (where the subject directly performs the action) rather than passive voice (where the subject receives the action instead), unless there's a specific reason to keep it passive, such as when the doer of the action is unknown or unimportant. Finally, when a question asks you to pick the best transition word, match it carefully to the actual relationship between the two ideas — is it a contrast, a result, an addition, or an example?

Explanation

Wordiness and vague language tend to creep into writing simply because we're used to speaking casually in everyday conversation, but formal or academic writing generally rewards being direct and specific instead. Active voice tends to read more clearly because it always tells you exactly who is doing what, while passive voice can sometimes hide that information or make a sentence feel a little more distant and roundabout. The central skill being tested throughout this whole topic is really just one simple question, applied over and over: "Can this idea be expressed in fewer, clearer words, without losing any of its original meaning?"

1. Make this concise: "Due to the fact that traffic was heavy, we arrived late."
"Because traffic was heavy, we arrived late."
2. Choose the best transition: "The team trained rigorously every day; ___, they still lost in the finals."
a) therefore b) however c) for example
b) however — signals contrast (they trained hard, but still lost).
3. Rewrite in formal tone: "The teacher was kinda annoyed with the noisy class."
"The teacher was somewhat annoyed with the noisy class." (or "visibly annoyed")
4. Which sentence is more precise? a) "The weather was bad during the trip." b) "Heavy rains and strong winds disrupted the trip."
b) — more specific and precise word choice.
5. Convert to active voice: "The award was given to her by the committee."
"The committee gave her the award."

English Vocabulary (High-Yield)

A4 — English Vocabulary (High-Yield)

▼
Meaning

Vocabulary questions on the UPCAT rarely test pure memorization of a long word list. Instead, they check whether you can work out the meaning of an unfamiliar word using clues in the surrounding sentence, or by recognizing familiar word parts — roots, prefixes, and suffixes — that show up again and again across many different English words. This makes vocabulary less about memorizing and more about developing a flexible, reusable skill.

Examples
  • Definition clue: "Her loquacious nature, or tendency to talk a lot, made her popular." (the meaning is basically handed to you, right there in the sentence)
  • Contrast clue: "Unlike her taciturn brother, she never stopped talking." (an opposite word nearby — "never stopped talking" — hints that taciturn means quiet or reserved)
  • Example clue: "Cephalopods, such as octopuses and squids, are highly intelligent creatures." (the specific examples given help you understand what category of animal is being described)
  • Root: "bio" (life) → biology (study of life), biography (writing about someone's life)
  • Prefix: "trans-" (across) → transport (carry across), transatlantic (across the Atlantic)
  • Collocation: "conduct research" (the natural, correct pairing) vs. "make research" (sounds logical, but is not how native speakers actually phrase it)
Application

When you come across an unfamiliar word, first look closely at the surrounding sentence — is the meaning explained directly, hinted at through an example, or suggested through an opposite word nearby? If there's no obvious clue in the sentence, try breaking the word itself into smaller parts: does it contain a root, prefix, or suffix that you already recognize from other words you know? For collocation-style questions, keep in mind that certain word pairings simply become "the accepted way" native English speakers phrase things, even if a different combination might seem just as logical or grammatically fine on paper.

Explanation

The exam tests vocabulary this particular way because it mirrors real, everyday reading — in real life, you won't always know every single word you come across, but you can usually still work out its approximate meaning using the context around it or the word's structure. Practicing common roots, prefixes, and suffixes essentially gives you a reusable toolkit that you can apply to completely new, unfamiliar words you've never seen before, which turns out to be a far more powerful and lasting strategy than trying to memorize one long, fixed vocabulary list.

1. "The professor's loquacious lecturing style meant class always ran overtime because he simply couldn't stop talking." What does "loquacious" mean?
Talkative/wordy (definition clue given directly in the sentence).
2. Root "cred" relates to: a) believe b) write c) people
a) believe (e.g., credible, credit)
3. Which sentence uses the correct collocation? a) "She made a research on frogs." b) "She conducted research on frogs."
b) — "conduct research" is the correct collocation.
4. Identify the connotation: which word has a more negative connotation — "assertive" or "pushy"? (Both can describe someone who states opinions firmly.)
"Pushy" has the more negative connotation.
5. Prefix "trans-" means: a) under b) across c) before
b) across (e.g., transport, transatlantic)

Filipino / Komunikasyon

A5.1 — Wastong Gamit ng Salita at Gramatika

▼
Meaning

Sinusuri ng paksang ito kung nagagamit mo nang tama ang mga salita at panandang Filipino tulad ng "ng/nang" at "may/mayroon," pati na rin kung nauunawaan mo ang aspekto (kung kailan naganap ang kilos) at pokus (kung sino/ano ang binibigyang-diin) ng pandiwa. Bagama't kilala natin ang mga salitang ito dahil ginagamit natin ito araw-araw, iba ang kailangan kapag sinusuri ito nang pormal sa isang pagsusulit — kailangang alam mo ang eksaktong tuntunin, hindi lang ang "pakiramdam" kung ano ang tama.

Halimbawa
  • Aspekto: "kumakain" (imperpektibo, ginagawa ngayon o paulit-ulit) vs. "kakain" (kontemplatibo, gagawin pa lang) vs. "kumain" (perpektibo, tapos na)
  • Pokus: "Kumain si Maria ng kanin." (diin kay Maria, ang gumawa ng kilos — pokus sa tagaganap) vs. "Kinain ni Maria ang kanin." (diin sa kanin, ang binigyang-pansin — pokus sa layon)
  • "ng" laban sa "nang": "Bumili ng tinapay si Ana." (ang "ng" dito ay panandang pambalana, nagpapakita ng layon ng bumili) vs. "Sumigaw siya nang malakas." (ang "nang" dito ay naglalarawan kung paano sumigaw — parang pang-abay)
  • "May" laban sa "mayroon": "May pagkain sa mesa." (agad-agad sinundan ng pangngalang "pagkain," walang ibang salita sa pagitan) vs. "Mayroon akong pagkain." (maaaring may salitang "akong" sa pagitan bago ang pangngalan)
  • "Rin/din": "Pumunta rin siya sa palengke." (gamit ang "rin" dahil ang naunang salitang "siya" ay nagtatapos sa patinig na "a")
Aplikasyon

Kapag nagbabasa ng pangungusap, tingnan muna kung nagsasabi ito ng tapos na kilos, ginagawa pa, o gagawin pa lang — iyan ang aspekto ng pandiwa. Pagkatapos, alamin kung sino o ano talaga ang binibigyang-diin ng buong pangungusap — kung ang sagot ay ang gumagawa ng kilos, ito ay pokus sa tagaganap; kung ang binibigyang-diin ay ang bagay na tinatamaan ng kilos, ito ay pokus sa layon. Para naman sa "ng" laban sa "nang," gamitin ang madaling pagsubok: tanungin sa sarili kung ang salita ay nagpapakita ng pagmamay-ari o layon (gamitin ang "ng"), o kung ito ay sumasagot sa tanong na "paano" o "kailan" ginawa ang kilos (gamitin ang "nang").

Paliwanag

Karaniwang nalilito ang mga mag-aaral sa mga salitang ito dahil magkatulad ang tunog kapag binibigkas — halos hindi mahahalata ang pagkakaiba ng "ng" at "nang" sa pandinig, gayundin ang "may" at "mayroon." Ang susi upang matandaan ang tamang gamit ay tandaan ang gawain ng bawat salita sa halip na ang tunog nito: ang "ng" ay palaging kasunod agad ng pangalan o layon ng kilos, samantalang ang "nang" ay sumasagot sa tanong na "paano" o "kailan." Sa parehong paraan, ang "may" ay hindi kailanman nag-iisa sa isang pangungusap — laging may kasunod na salita agad, habang ang "mayroon" ay mas flexible at maaaring paghiwalayin ng ibang salita bago ang pangngalan.

b) imperpektibo — nagpapahiwatig ng nagaganap/paulit-ulit na kilos.

a) ng b) nang
b) nang — naglalarawan kung paano umalis (pang-abay).

b) layon — nakatuon ang diin sa "bag," ang binili.

A5.2 — Talasalitaan at Panitikan (High-Yield)

▼
Meaning

Ang paksang ito ay tungkol sa paghula ng kahulugan ng salita mula sa konteksto (katulad ng ginagawa natin sa Ingles), at pagkilala sa iba't ibang uri ng tayutay — mga paraan ng matalinghagang pagsasalita na karaniwang ginagamit sa panitikan upang gawing mas buhay at mas makapangyarihan ang isang akda, sa halip na sabihin lamang ang literal na kahulugan nito nang tuwiran.

Halimbawa
  • Pagtutulad (simile) — direktang paghahambing gamit ang pananda: "Kasing puti ng niyebe ang balat niya." (may pananda: "kasing")
  • Pagwawangis (metaphor) — hindi direktang paghahambing, walang pananda: "Siya ay bulaklak sa tahanan." (sinabing SIYA mismo ang bulaklak, hindi lang "parang" bulaklak)
  • Pagmamalabis (hyperbole) — labis na pagpapalaki ng bagay upang bigyang-diin ang damdamin: "Namatay na ako sa gutom!" (hindi literal, ipinapahayag lang ang matinding gutom)
  • Pagbibigay-katauhan (personification) — binibigyan ng katangiang tao ang bagay o hayop: "Umiiyak ang langit." (ang langit ay hindi talaga umiiyak, pero binigyan ito ng damdaming pantao)
  • Idyoma: "nagbibilang ng bituin" (ibig sabihin: walang ginagawa o nag-aaksaya ng oras — hindi ito literal na pagbibilang ng mga bituin sa langit)
Aplikasyon

Kapag nakakita ka ng matalinghagang pangungusap, tingnan muna kung mayroong salitang pananda tulad ng "parang," "tulad ng," o "kasing" — kung mayroon, malamang ay pagtutulad ito. Kung walang ganitong pananda pero direkta namang inihahambing ang dalawang bagay bilang iisa, malamang ay pagwawangis ito. Kung ang isang bagay ay labis na pinalaki nang hindi talaga totoo sa katunayan, ito ay pagmamalabis, at kung ang isang bagay o hayop ay ginawang parang may kakayahang gawin ang isang bagay na tao lang talaga ang kayang gawin (tulad ng umiyak o magsalita), ito ay pagbibigay-katauhan.

Paliwanag

Ang mga tayutay ay ginagamit ng mga manunulat upang gawing mas kapana-panabik at mas madama ng mambabasa ang isang akda, sa halip na sabihin lamang ang eksaktong literal na kahulugan nito, na kadalasang mas mapapadama sa atin ang emosyon o larawan na gustong ihatid ng may-akda. Ang pagkilala sa uri ng tayutay ay nangangailangan ng masusing pagbabasa sa buong pangungusap — hindi lamang sa iisang salita — dahil ang buong konteksto ang magsasabi sa atin kung ano talaga ang ipinapahiwatig nito, at kung minsan, magkatulad ang dating ng dalawang uri ng tayutay kaya kailangan talagang basahing mabuti ang pangungusap.

1. Anong tayutay ang ginamit sa "Ang mga bituin ay kumindat sa akin buong gabi"?
a) simile b) personipikasyon c) ironiya
b) personipikasyon — binigyan ng kilos-taong "pagkindat" ang mga bituin.
2. Ano ang kahulugan ng sawikaing "may isang kahig, isang tuka"?
Kumikita nang kaunti lamang, sapat na sapat lang para sa pang-araw-araw na pangangailangan.
3. Anong tayutay ang "Ang tinig niya ay musika sa aking pandinig" (walang "parang/tulad ng")?
a) pagtutulad b) pagwawangis c) pagmamalabis
b) pagwawangis (metaphor) — direktang inihambing ang tinig sa musika nang walang pananda.
4. Anong uri ng teksto ang naglalarawan ng hitsura o katangian ng isang bagay/tao nang detalyado?
a) naratibo b) deskriptibo c) argumentatibo
b) deskriptibo
5. Ano ang ipinahihiwatig ng idyomang "nasa dulo ng dila"?
Malapit nang masabi o naaalala pero hindi pa masabi/maalala nang eksakto.

▼
Meaning

Ang paksang ito ay tungkol sa pagkilala kung anong uri ng teksto ang binabasa mo — batay sa layunin nito (naratibo, deskriptibo, argumentatibo, o impormatibo) — at pagkilala rin sa mga karaniwang klase ng sulatin na maaaring lumabas sa pagsusulit, tulad ng sanaysay, talumpati, at balita.   • Naratibo — nagkukuwento ng mga pangyayari, may simula, gitna, at wakas: "Isang umaga, habang naglalakad si Juan patungong paaralan..."

  • Talumpati — isang sulatin na binabasa nang malakas sa harap ng tagapakinig, may layuning humikayat o magbigay-impormasyon.

Ang pagkilala sa uri ng teksto at klase ng sulatin ay mahalaga dahil naiiba ang paraan ng pagsulat at layunin ng bawat isa — halimbawa, hindi mo aasahan ang personal na opinyon sa isang impormatibong teksto, habang inaasahan mo ito sa isang sanaysay. Question Types to Master

B1 — Question Types to Master

▼
Meaning

Reading comprehension questions aren't all testing the same skill. Some ask what the passage directly says, some ask what it implies without stating it outright, and some ask you to judge the author's purpose, tone, or argument structure. Knowing which type of question you're facing before you start searching for the answer tells you exactly where to look and how deeply you need to think — a detail question needs a quick scan, while an inference question needs careful reasoning.

The full list of question types you'll see
  • Main idea / central thesis — "What is the passage mainly about?" (tests whether you understood the passage as a whole)
  • Best summary of the passage — similar to main idea, but asks you to pick the answer choice that captures everything important without leaving out a major part or adding something not in the passage. A common trap is a summary that's technically true but too narrow (only covers part of the passage) or too broad (adds ideas the passage never mentioned).
  • Supporting detail (explicit) — "According to the passage, what happened in 1990?" (the answer is stated directly somewhere in the text)
  • Inference — "What does the passage suggest, though it isn't directly stated?" (the answer isn't written word-for-word, but is strongly implied)
  • Author's purpose — "Why did the author write this passage?" (to inform, persuade, criticize, or explain)
  • Tone/attitude — "What is the author's tone?" (neutral, critical, hopeful, ironic — this is about how something is said, not just what is said)
  • Argument structure (claim–evidence–reasoning) — asks you to identify the writer's main claim, the evidence given to support it, and the reasoning that connects the evidence to the claim. Some questions will ask you to pick out which sentence is the claim versus which is supporting evidence.
  • Assumptions (unstated but necessary) — an assumption is something the author must believe to be true for their argument to work, even though they never actually say it outright. To find one, ask: "What would have to be true for this argument to make sense?"
  • Cause-effect relationships — "What caused the event described?" (tests whether you can trace why something happened)
  • Compare-contrast relationships — "How are X and Y different/similar?" (tests whether you can track two or more ideas side by side)
  • Problem-solution structure — some passages are organized around presenting a problem and then proposing one or more solutions; questions may ask you to identify the problem, the proposed solution, or evaluate whether the solution actually addresses the problem.
  • Meaning of a word/phrase in context — asks what a specific word means as it's used in this particular passage, which may be different from its most common dictionary definition. Always re-read the sentence around the word rather than relying only on what you think the word usually means.
  • Best title / best concluding statement — asks you to pick the option that best captures the passage as a whole, similar to "best summary," but usually shorter and more general.
  • EXCEPT/NOT/LEAST questions — "All of the following are true EXCEPT..." (a trap question type where three answers are correct and only one is not)
Application

Read the question before you re-read the passage, so you already know exactly what to hunt for as you go back through the text. If it's a detail question, scan for exact facts, names, dates, or numbers rather than reading every word again. If it's an inference question, look for what's strongly implied by the passage even though it isn't written in those exact words. For argument structure and assumption questions, separate the passage mentally into "what the author is claiming," "what evidence they give," and "what they must be assuming is true but never actually say." For word-in-context questions, always re-read the full sentence (and maybe the sentence before and after), since the specific context can shift a word's meaning away from its most common definition. For EXCEPT/NOT/LEAST questions, check each answer choice one at a time against the passage; three of them will be true or supported by the text, and exactly one will not be.

Explanation

The exam deliberately mixes these question types together because each one tests a slightly different reading skill: literal understanding (can you find a stated fact), interpretation (can you understand what's implied), or judgment (can you evaluate the author's intent, argument, or attitude). Practicing to recognize the question type before you go searching for the answer saves valuable time during the actual test, and it also prevents a very common mistake — confusing "what the passage actually says" with "what you personally think about the topic."

Sample Passage (for practice)

"When the local government proposed replacing the old public market with a modern shopping complex, many longtime vendors protested. They argued that the market had served the community for generations and that a shopping complex would raise rental costs beyond what small vendors could afford. Supporters of the project, however, claimed that the new complex would attract more customers and improve sanitation. After months of debate, the government agreed to preserve a section of the market for traditional vendors while building the new complex around it."

1. What is the main idea of the passage?
A compromise was reached between preserving the traditional market and building a modern complex, after vendors protested the original proposal.
2. Why did the vendors protest, according to the passage? (supporting detail)
They worried that a shopping complex would raise rental costs beyond what small vendors could afford.
3. What can you infer about the government's final decision? (inference)
The government tried to balance both sides' concerns rather than fully siding with either the vendors or the developers.
4. What is the author's tone in this passage?
a) strongly biased toward vendors b) neutral/balanced c) mocking
b) neutral/balanced — presents both sides without taking an obvious side.
5. All of the following are true EXCEPT:
a) Vendors protested the proposal b) The market was completely demolished c) A compromise was reached
b) — the passage says a section was preserved, so it was not completely demolished.
6. What claim do the supporters of the project make, and what evidence do they give for it? (argument structure)
Claim: the new complex would benefit the community. Evidence: it would attract more customers and improve sanitation.
7. For the vendors' argument to make sense, what must they be assuming? (assumption)
They must be assuming that rental costs in the new complex would indeed be too high for them to continue operating — an assumption about the future that isn't directly proven in the passage, just stated as their belief.
8. Which best describes the structure of this passage? (problem-solution)
a) A problem is presented, opposing views are given, then a solution/compromise is reached
b) A single solution is proposed with no opposing views
c) The passage only describes a historical event with no problem or solution

Skills & Techniques

B2 — Skills & Techniques

▼
Meaning

Beyond simply knowing question types, strong readers rely on specific techniques to move through a passage efficiently instead of reading every single word at the same careful pace. This includes skimming for the overall structure, scanning quickly for specific details, noticing "signal words" that reveal how ideas are connected, and — for more advanced passages — evaluating the quality of evidence or reading information presented alongside a graph or table.

Examples
  • Skimming: quickly reading topic sentences (usually the first sentence of each paragraph) to get a sense of the passage's overall structure and main points, without reading every supporting detail yet.
  • Scanning: searching quickly through the text for a specific name, date, number, or keyword, without reading every word around it carefully.
  • Shift words: words like "however," "although," "on the other hand," and "nevertheless" signal a change in direction — whenever you see one of these, it's worth slowing down, since that's often exactly where the key argument, contrast, or twist in the passage happens.
  • Reference questions: vague words like "this," "that," "such," or "it" almost always refer back to an idea mentioned in a nearby sentence — always trace these back to the exact noun or idea they're pointing to before answering a question about them.
  • Fact vs. opinion vs. interpretation: a fact can be checked/proven ("The event happened in 1990"), an opinion is a personal judgment ("The event was unfortunate"), and an interpretation is a reasonable conclusion drawn from evidence, but not directly stated.
  • Spotting and evaluating evidence: when an author makes a claim, check what specific evidence (statistics, examples, expert opinions) they use to back it up, and whether that evidence actually relates closely to the claim, or only loosely connects to it.
  • Paired text + graph/table items: some passages come with an accompanying chart, graph, or table. Always read the axis labels, units, and title of the graph first, before trying to match it to what the passage says, since the two sources are usually meant to be read together, with the graph adding data that supports or extends the written text.
Application

Before diving into the questions, skim the passage once from start to finish to understand its general shape. When a question asks about one specific detail, scan for it directly instead of re-reading the entire passage from the beginning. Whenever you notice a shift word like "however" or "but," pay closer attention right at that point in the text. When a question uses a vague word like "this" or "it," trace it back carefully to the exact noun or idea it refers to. When a question asks you to evaluate an author's evidence, check specifically whether that evidence is directly relevant to the claim being made, or whether it's only tangentially related — passages sometimes include evidence that sounds convincing but doesn't actually prove the exact point being argued. When a graph or table accompanies a passage, read its labels and units first, then check whether the passage's claims are actually supported by what the graph or table shows.

Explanation

These techniques exist because reading comprehension is partly a time-management skill, not just a comprehension skill — you simply can't afford to read every single word of every passage with equal, careful attention within a limited time. Skimming and scanning let you use your reading time strategically: get a quick, general understanding first, and then go back for focused, detailed searching only when a specific question actually requires it. Evaluating evidence and reading graphs alongside text are slightly more advanced skills that test whether you can connect multiple sources of information together, rather than treating the written passage as the only source of truth.

1. What is the difference between skimming and scanning?
Skimming is reading quickly for overall structure/idea; scanning is searching quickly for a specific detail (like a name or number).
2. In the sentence "The plan seemed promising; however, it failed within a year," what does "however" signal?
A contrast or change in direction — a positive expectation followed by a negative outcome.
3. In "The mayor cut the ribbon at the ceremony. This marked the opening of the new library," what does "this" refer to?
The act of cutting the ribbon at the ceremony.
4. True or False: You should always read every word of a passage carefully before looking at the questions.
False — skimming first for structure, then scanning for details as needed, is more time-efficient.
5. Which is a "shift word" that signals contrast? a) moreover b) although c) therefore
b) although
6. A passage claims that "reading before bed improves sleep quality," and supports this with a quote from one person's personal experience. Is this strong or weak evidence, and why?
Weak evidence — a single personal experience doesn't prove a general claim; stronger evidence would involve a study or data across many people.
7. A passage discusses rising unemployment and includes a bar graph showing unemployment rates over five years. What should you check first before answering questions about the graph?
The graph's axis labels and units (e.g., what years, what percentage scale) to correctly understand what the data actually shows.

Passage Types to Practice

B3 — Passage Types to Practice

▼
Meaning

UPCAT reading passages come in several different types — science/tech explanations, social science texts, editorials, narratives, and argumentative essays — and each type tends to be organized and written a little differently. Learning to recognize the type of passage you're reading, right from the first few sentences, helps you predict its structure and anticipate the kind of questions that are likely to follow.

Examples
  • Science/tech passages: usually explain a process, experiment, or phenomenon step by step, often in a fairly neutral, factual tone.
  • Social science texts: discuss historical, economic, or sociological topics, often exploring cause-effect relationships or comparing different time periods, places, or groups.
  • Editorials/opinion pieces: present a clear argument or personal opinion, often using persuasive language and rhetorical techniques to convince the reader.
  • Narrative nonfiction/biography: tells a story about real events or a real person's life, usually organized in chronological (time) order, and often includes more personal or descriptive language.
  • Argumentative essays/policy pieces: present a specific claim, supporting evidence for that claim, and often directly address counterarguments or opposing viewpoints.
Application

When you start reading a new passage, quickly ask yourself: is this describing a process or explaining how something works (science/tech), telling a story about real people or events (narrative), or arguing for a particular position (editorial/argumentative)? Recognizing this early helps you anticipate what kind of questions will likely follow — process-based passages tend to have more detail and sequence questions, while argumentative passages tend to have more "author's purpose," "tone," and "strengthen/weaken the argument" type questions.

Explanation

The type of passage genuinely affects both its writing style and the kind of questions the exam is likely to build around it. A science passage, for example, is more likely to be tested through fact-based and sequence-based questions, since its main goal is to explain something clearly and accurately. An opinion piece, on the other hand, is more likely to be tested through questions about tone, purpose, and argument structure, since its main goal is to persuade the reader toward a particular viewpoint. Recognizing the passage type early on helps you read with the right expectations and focus your attention on the details that are most likely to actually be tested.

Science/tech explanation.
2. A passage arguing that plastic bags should be banned, using statistics and addressing opposing views, is what type?
Argumentative essay / policy piece.
3. A passage recounting a scientist's childhood and career milestones is what type?
Narrative nonfiction / biography.
4. Which passage type is most likely to use persuasive language and a personal opinion?
a) social science text b) editorial/opinion column c) science explanation
b) editorial/opinion column
5. A passage discussing the economic effects of a historical event, with cause-effect analysis, is what type?
Social science text.

Number Sense & Arithmetic

C1 — Number Sense & Arithmetic

▼
Meaning

This covers the basic building blocks of math: how whole numbers, integers, fractions, decimals, and percents behave, plus quick number facts like divisibility, LCM/GCF, exponents, and roots. These skills quietly show up inside almost every other math topic on the exam, so they need to become automatic rather than something you stop and think hard about each time.

Examples
  • Integers and absolute value: −7 is an integer; its absolute value |−7| = 7 (absolute value is always the distance from zero, so it's never negative)
  • Order of operations (PEMDAS): 3 + 2 × 4 = 3 + 8 = 11 (multiply/divide before adding/subtracting, left to right)
  • Fractions: to add 1/4 + 1/6, find a common denominator (12): 3/12 + 2/12 = 5/12; to convert 7/2 (improper) to a mixed number: 3½
  • Decimals: 0.35 means 35 hundredths; adding decimals requires lining up the decimal points first
  • Percent change: price from ₱100 to ₱120 → (20/100) × 100 = 20%
  • Ratios and proportions: if a recipe uses a 2:3 ratio of sugar to flour, and you use 4 cups of sugar, you'd need 6 cups of flour (scale both sides equally)
  • Rates: speed is a rate — distance per unit of time (km per hour)
  • LCM/GCF: the LCM of 4 and 6 is 12 (smallest number both divide into evenly); the GCF of 12 and 18 is 6 (largest number that divides into both)
  • Divisibility rule: a number is divisible by 3 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 3 (e.g., 123 → 1+2+3=6, divisible by 3)
  • Exponent law: x² × x³ = x⁵ (add exponents when multiplying same base)
  • Roots/radicals: √16 = 4; √50 simplifies to 5√2 (pull out perfect square factors)
  • Scientific notation: 45,000 = 4.5 × 10⁴
  • Estimation: rounding 48 × 22 to about 50 × 20 = 1,000 gives you a quick reasonableness check on the real answer
Application

Whenever a problem involves a percent, ratio, ora ratio, or exponent, go back to the basic definition rather than guessing: percent change is always (change ÷ original) × 100, and a ratio compares two quantities directly. For fractions, always find a common denominator before adding or subtracting, and simplify your final answer. For LCM/GCF, use prime factorization if the numbers are large — break each number into its prime factors, then compare. For divisibility and prime factorization questions, use the shortcut rules instead of long division. Use estimation to sanity-check your final answer on any calculation-heavy question, especially under time pressure.

Explanation

Number sense questions are designed to be solved quickly using rules and shortcuts, not long calculations. Practicing these rules until they're automatic frees up more time for the harder algebra and geometry problems later in the exam. Estimation in particular is a habit worth building early, since it catches careless errors (like a misplaced decimal point) before they cost you points.

1. Simplify: 6 + 4 × (2 + 3) = ?
6 + 4×5 = 6 + 20 = 26
2. Add: 2/3 + 1/4
Common denominator 12: 8/12 + 3/12 = 11/12
3. A shirt's price increased from ₱250 to ₱300. What is the percent increase?
(50/250) × 100 = 20%
4. Find the LCM and GCF of 8 and 12.
LCM = 24, GCF = 4
5. Simplify √72.
√72 = √(36×2) = 6√2

C2.1 — Algebra — Expressions, Equations, and Inequalities

▼
Meaning

This is about translating word problems into math expressions, simplifying them, and solving for an unknown value using equations or inequalities — including equations and inequalities that involve absolute value, and systems where more than one inequality must be satisfied at once.

Examples
  • Translating words: "five more than twice a number" → 2x + 5
  • Simplifying: 3x + 5x − 2 = 8x − 2 (combine like terms)
  • Solving a linear equation: 2x + 4 = 10 → 2x = 6 → x = 3
  • Solving an inequality: 3x − 2 > 7 → 3x > 9 → x > 3 (sign flips only when multiplying/dividing by a negative)
  • Absolute value equation: |x − 3| = 5 → x − 3 = 5 or x − 3 = −5 → x = 8 or x = −2 (absolute value equations split into two cases)
  • Absolute value inequality: |x| < 4 → −4 < x < 4 (the solution is a range between two values)
  • Systems of equations: solved using substitution or elimination to find values that satisfy both equations at once.
  • Systems of inequalities: the solution is the overlapping region where both inequalities are true at the same time, often shown as a shaded area on a graph.
Application

When translating a word problem, look for keywords: "sum/more than" (+), "difference/less than" (−), "product/times" (×), "quotient/divided by" (÷). When solving an equation, do the same operation to both sides to isolate the variable. Remember: when you multiply or divide both sides of an inequality by a negative number, flip the inequality sign. For absolute value equations, always split into two separate cases (positive and negative) before solving each one. For systems of inequalities, think of each inequality as defining a region on a graph, and the answer is wherever those regions overlap.

Explanation

Algebra questions are really just puzzles: an unknown value is hidden, and equations are the rules that let you uncover it logically, one step at a time. Absolute value problems trip students up because they represent distance from zero, which is always positive — that's exactly why they split into two possible cases. Practicing the translation step (words → math) is often what separates a quick solve from a confusing one.

1. Translate: "Ten less than three times a number is 20." Write the equation.
3x − 10 = 20
2. Solve: 4x − 7 = 13
4x = 20 → x = 5
3. Solve the absolute value equation: |x + 2| = 7
x + 2 = 7 or x + 2 = −7 → x = 5 or x = −9
4. Solve the system: x + y = 10, x − y = 4
Adding both equations: 2x = 14 → x = 7, then y = 3
5. Solve the inequality: −2x + 3 < 9
−2x < 6 → x > −3 (sign flips because we divide by a negative)

C2.2 — Functions and Linear Equations

▼
Meaning

A function is a rule that takes an input and produces exactly one output. This topic covers function notation, how to read and use linear functions (straight-line equations), and how to determine the domain and range of a function from its equation or graph.

Examples
  • Function notation: f(x) = 2x + 3 means "the function f, applied to x, gives 2x + 3." So f(4) = 2(4) + 3 = 11 (substitute 4 in place of x)
  • Linear function, slope-intercept form: y = mx + b, where m is the slope (steepness) and b is the y-intercept (where the line crosses the y-axis)
  • Domain: the set of all possible input (x) values a function can accept
  • Range: the set of all possible output (y) values a function can produce
  • Reading a graph for domain/range: if a graph only shows points from x = 0 to x = 10, the domain is 0 ≤ x ≤ 10
Application

When you see function notation like f(3), treat it as "plug 3 in for every x in the function's rule." For linear functions in slope-intercept form, immediately identify the slope (how steep, and whether it rises or falls) and the y-intercept (the starting point on the y-axis) — these two numbers tell you everything you need to graph the line quickly. For domain and range questions based on a graph, look at how far left/right the graph extends (domain) and how far up/down it extends (range).

Explanation

Function notation can look intimidating at first, but it's really just a labeling system — f(x) is just another way of writing "y," with the added benefit of showing exactly which variable you're plugging values into. Slope-intercept form is useful precisely because it puts the two most important pieces of information about a line — its steepness and its starting point — right where you can see them immediately, without needing to do any extra calculation.

1. If f(x) = 3x − 5, find f(4).
f(4) = 3(4) − 5 = 12 − 5 = 7
2. Identify the slope and y-intercept of y = −2x + 7.
slope = −2, y-intercept = 7
3. If f(x) = x² + 1, find f(−2).
f(−2) = (−2)² + 1 = 4 + 1 = 5
4. A graph shows a line existing only between x = −3 and x = 5. What is its domain?
−3 ≤ x ≤ 5
5. Write the equation of a line with slope 4 and y-intercept −1.
y = 4x − 1

C2.3 — Polynomials, Factoring, and Quadratics

▼
Meaning

This covers operations on polynomial expressions (adding, subtracting, multiplying), breaking them down into factors, recognizing special factoring patterns, and solving quadratic equations (equations where the variable is squared), including a basic understanding of what their graphs look like.

Examples
  • Adding/subtracting polynomials: (3x² + 2x) + (x² − 5x) = 4x² − 3x (combine like terms)
  • Multiplying polynomials: (x + 2)(x + 3) = x² + 5x + 6 (use FOIL: First, Outer, Inner, Last)
  • Factoring GCF: 6x² + 9x = 3x(2x + 3)
  • Difference of squares: x² − 9 = (x − 3)(x + 3)
  • Perfect square trinomial: x² + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)² (recognize when the first and last terms are perfect squares and the middle term fits the pattern 2ab)
  • Factoring by grouping: x³ + 3x² + 2x + 6 = x²(x + 3) + 2(x + 3) = (x + 3)(x² + 2) (group terms in pairs, factor each pair, then factor out the common binomial)
  • Factoring a trinomial: x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3)
  • Solving by factoring: x² − 5x + 6 = 0 → (x−2)(x−3) = 0 → x = 2 or x = 3
  • Quadratic formula: x = (−b ± √(b²−4ac)) / 2a
  • Graph basics of y = ax² + bx + c: the graph is a U-shaped curve called a parabola; the vertex is its highest or lowest point, and the x-intercepts (if any) are where the parabola crosses the x-axis — these are the same as the solutions you'd get from factoring or the quadratic formula.
Application

When multiplying two binomials, use FOIL to make sure you multiply every term in the first group by every term in the second. Before using the quadratic formula, always try factoring first — it's usually faster. To factor a trinomial x² + bx + c, look for two numbers that multiply to c and add to b. Recognize a perfect square trinomial by checking if the first and last terms are perfect squares. For factoring by grouping, split the expression into two pairs of terms, factor each pair separately, and see if a common binomial factor appears. When asked about a parabola's graph, remember that its x-intercepts are exactly the solutions to the quadratic equation set equal to zero.

Explanation

Factoring is essentially "undoing" multiplication, which is why recognizing patterns (like difference of squares or perfect square trinomials) speeds things up dramatically. The connection between factoring, the quadratic formula, and a parabola's graph is not a coincidence — all three approaches are really answering the same question ("where does this expression equal zero?") from different angles, so understanding one helps you understand the others.

1. Multiply: (x + 4)(x − 2)
x² + 2x − 8 (using FOIL)
2. Factor: x² − 16
(x − 4)(x + 4)
3. Factor the perfect square trinomial: x² + 10x + 25
(x + 5)²
4. Solve by factoring: x² − x − 6 = 0
(x − 3)(x + 2) = 0 → x = 3 or x = −2
5. A parabola y = x² − 4 crosses the x-axis at which points?
Set x² − 4 = 0 → (x−2)(x+2) = 0 → x = 2 or x = −2, so it crosses at (2,0) and (−2,0)

C2.4 — Rational, Exponential, and Logarithmic Basics

▼
Meaning

This covers expressions with variables in a fraction (rational expressions), quantities that grow or shrink by a consistent percentage over time (exponential growth/decay), and the basic idea of a logarithm as the "reverse" of an exponent.

Examples
  • Rational expression: (x² − 4)/(x − 2) can be simplified by factoring the top: (x−2)(x+2)/(x−2) = x + 2 (as long as x ≠ 2, since that would make the original denominator zero)
  • Restrictions: for any rational expression, the denominator can never equal zero — so you must exclude any x-value that would make it zero.
  • Exponential growth: a population growing by 5% each year can be modeled as P = P₀(1.05)ᵗ, where P₀ is the starting population and t is time in years.
  • Exponential decay: similar idea, but the growth factor is less than 1 (like 0.95 for a 5% decrease each year).
  • Logarithm basics: a logarithm answers the question "what exponent do I need?" For example, log₂8 = 3, because 2³ = 8. In other words, a logarithm is just the reverse operation of an exponent.
Application

For rational expressions, always factor first if possible, since this often lets you cancel common factors and simplify. Always state any restrictions (values that would make the denominator zero) as part of your answer. For exponential growth/decay word problems, identify the starting amount, the growth or decay rate, and the time period, then plug them into the general form P = P₀(rate)ᵗ. For simple logarithm questions, convert the log expression into its equivalent exponential form to make it easier to solve — logₐb = c is the same statement as aᶜ = b.

Explanation

Rational expressions behave like regular fractions, just with variables involved, so the same rules apply — you can't divide by zero, and simplifying by factoring works the same way. Exponential growth and decay show up constantly in real life (population, interest rates, radioactive decay), which is why recognizing the pattern "grows/shrinks by a consistent percentage" is such a useful skill. Logarithms often feel abstract at first, but remembering that they're simply asking "what power do I need" makes them much more approachable.

1. Simplify: (x² − 9)/(x − 3), and state the restriction.
(x−3)(x+3)/(x−3) = x + 3, where x ≠ 3
2. A population of 1,000 grows by 10% each year. Write the exponential growth expression for the population after t years.
P = 1000(1.10)ᵗ
3. Evaluate log₃9.
log₃9 = 2, because 3² = 9
4. Simplify: (2x)/(4x²), and state any restriction.
1/(2x), where x ≠ 0
5. A car worth ₱500,000 depreciates (loses value) by 8% per year. Write the exponential decay expression.
V = 500000(0.92)ᵗ

C2.5 — Sequences and Variation

▼
Meaning

This covers patterns of numbers that follow a consistent rule (sequences), and relationships between two quantities where one changes in a predictable way based on the other (variation).

Examples
  • Arithmetic sequence: a sequence where each term increases (or decreases) by the same fixed amount, called the common difference. Example: 3, 7, 11, 15, ... (common difference = 4)
  • Geometric sequence: a sequence where each term is multiplied by the same fixed amount, called the common ratio. Example: 2, 6, 18, 54, ... (common ratio = 3)
  • Direct variation: y = kx, meaning y increases proportionally as x increases (if x doubles, y doubles too). k is called the constant of variation.
  • Inverse variation: y = k/x, meaning as x increases, y decreases proportionally (if x doubles, y is cut in half).
Application

For arithmetic sequences, find the common difference by subtracting any term from the one right after it; use this to find any missing term by repeatedly adding (or subtracting) that difference. For geometric sequences, find the common ratio by dividing any term by the one before it. To recognize direct vs. inverse variation in a word problem, ask: as one quantity increases, does the other one also increase (direct) or decrease (inverse)? Once you identify which type, set up the appropriate equation (y = kx or y = k/x) and solve for the constant k using given values.

Explanation

Sequences are really just patterns with a consistent, describable rule — once you find that rule (the common difference or common ratio), you can predict any term in the sequence without listing every single one. Variation problems are common in real-world word problems (like speed and travel time, or the relationship between the number of workers and time to finish a job), so recognizing whether a relationship is direct or inverse is a practical skill beyond just the exam.

1. Find the next term: 5, 9, 13, 17, ___
21 (arithmetic, common difference = 4)
2. Find the next term: 3, 6, 12, 24, ___
48 (geometric, common ratio = 2)
3. If y varies directly with x, and y = 20 when x = 4, find y when x = 10.
k = 20/4 = 5, so y = 5(10) = 50
4. If y varies inversely with x, and y = 6 when x = 2, find y when x = 3.
k = 6×2 = 12, so y = 12/3 = 4
5. Find the 10th term of the arithmetic sequence: 2, 5, 8, 11, ...
common difference = 3; 10th term = 2 + 9(3) = 29

Geometry & Measurement

C3.1 — Angles and Triangles

▼
Meaning

This covers the properties of angles and triangles — how their angles and sides relate to each other in fixed, predictable ways, including special angle pairs formed by intersecting or parallel lines, and the different ways triangles can be classified and compared.

Examples
  • Complementary angles add up to 90°; supplementary angles add up to 180°.
  • Vertical angles: formed when two lines cross each other; the angles directly across from each other are always equal. If two lines cross and one angle is 40°, the angle directly opposite it is also 40°.
  • Angles formed by parallel lines cut by a transversal: corresponding angles are equal, and alternate interior angles are equal.
  • The three angles inside any triangle always add up to exactly 180°.
  • Types of triangles: isosceles (two equal sides and two equal angles), equilateral (all three sides and angles equal), right triangle (one 90° angle)
  • Pythagorean theorem: a² + b² = c² (for right triangles, c is the hypotenuse)
  • Special right triangles: 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 have fixed, memorizable side ratios.
  • Triangle similarity: two triangles are similar if their corresponding angles are equal and their corresponding sides are proportional (same shape, different size).
Application

Whenever two lines cross, immediately identify the vertical angle pairs (equal to each other) and the supplementary pairs (adding to 180°). When parallel lines are cut by a transversal, use the corresponding and alternate interior angle rules to quickly find any missing angle without needing to calculate from scratch. When you see a right triangle, check if the Pythagorean theorem or a special triangle ratio applies. For triangle similarity questions, set up a proportion between corresponding sides of the two triangles to solve for an unknown length.

Explanation

Geometry rules exist because shapes follow consistent, provable relationships — once you know a triangle's angles always sum to 180°, or that vertical angles are always equal, you can find missing angles without measuring. Triangle similarity is essentially a scaled-up or scaled-down version of the same shape, which is why setting up a simple proportion between matching sides is enough to solve most similarity problems.

1. Two lines intersect, forming four angles. One angle measures 65°. What is the measure of the angle vertically opposite it?
65° (vertical angles are always equal)
2. In the same intersection, what is the measure of the angle adjacent (next to) the 65° angle?
180° − 65° = 115° (supplementary angles)
3. A right triangle has legs of 3 and 4. What is the hypotenuse?
3² + 4² = 9+16 = 25 → √25 = 5
4. Triangle ABC is similar to triangle DEF. If AB = 6, DE = 3, and BC = 10, what is EF?
Scale factor = 3/6 = 1/2, so EF = 10 × 1/2 = 5
5. In a triangle, two angles measure 50° and 60°. What is the third angle, and what type of triangle is it (based on angles)?
180° − 50° − 60° = 70°; since all three angles are less than 90°, it's an acute triangle.

C3.2 — Polygons and Quadrilaterals

▼
Meaning

This covers the specific properties of four-sided shapes (quadrilaterals) like parallelograms, rectangles, squares, rhombuses, and trapezoids, as well as general rules for finding the interior/exterior angles, perimeter, and area of polygons.

Examples
  • Parallelogram: opposite sides are equal and parallel, opposite angles are equal.
  • Rectangle: a parallelogram with four right angles.
  • Square: a rectangle with all four sides equal.
  • Rhombus: a parallelogram with all four sides equal (but angles don't have to be 90°).
  • Trapezoid: only one pair of parallel sides.
  • Sum of interior angles of a polygon: (n − 2) × 180°, where n = number of sides.
  • Perimeter: the total distance around a shape (add up all the side lengths).
  • Area formulas: rectangle A = length × width; square A = side²; parallelogram A = base × height; trapezoid A = ½(base₁ + base₂) × height
Application

When identifying a quadrilateral, check its defining properties one at a time: are opposite sides parallel? Are all angles 90°? Are all sides equal? This process of elimination usually narrows it down quickly. For polygon angle questions, use the formula (n−2)×180° rather than adding angles one by one. For perimeter, simply add every side length; for area, match the shape to its correct formula before calculating.

Explanation

Each quadrilateral type is really just a more specific version of a more general shape — a square is a special rectangle, and a rectangle is a special parallelogram — which is why their properties build on each other. Memorizing the small set of area formulas for common shapes covers the large majority of perimeter/area questions on the exam.

1. What is the sum of the interior angles of a hexagon (6 sides)?
(6−2)×180° = 720°
2. Find the area of a rectangle with length 8 and width 5.
8 × 5 = 40
3. Find the area of a trapezoid with parallel sides 6 and 10, and height 4.
½(6+10)(4) = ½(16)(4) = 32
4. A rhombus has all four sides equal but its angles are not 90°. Is it also a rectangle? Why or why not?
No — a rectangle requires four right angles, which this rhombus doesn't have.
5. Find the perimeter of a square with side length 7.
7 × 4 = 28

C3.3 — Circles, Coordinate Geometry, and Solid Geometry

▼
Meaning

This topic covers circle-related parts and formulas (radius, chord, circumference, area, arc length), how to work with points and lines on a coordinate plane, and how to calculate the volume and surface area of three-dimensional solids.

Examples
  • Circle parts: radius (center to edge), diameter (edge to edge through the center, twice the radius), chord (a line segment connecting two points on the circle), central angle (an angle with its vertex at the center)
  • Circumference: C = 2πr
  • Area of a circle: A = πr²
  • Arc length: a portion of the circumference, found using (central angle/360°) × circumference
  • Sector area: a "pizza slice" portion of the circle's area, found using (central angle/360°) × πr²
  • Slope formula: m = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁)
  • Midpoint formula: the midpoint between two points (x₁,y₁) and (x₂,y₂) is ((x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2) — essentially the average of the x-values and the average of the y-values
  • Distance formula: d = √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²]
  • Volume of a rectangular prism: V = length × width × height
  • Volume of a cylinder: V = πr²h
  • Surface area of a rectangular prism: SA = 2(lw + lh + wh) (the sum of the area of all six faces)
  • Cone, pyramid, sphere volumes (conceptual awareness): cone V = ⅓πr²h; pyramid V = ⅓ × base area × height; sphere V = (4/3)πr³
Application

For circle problems, identify whether the question asks for circumference, area, arc length, or sector area — arc length and sector area both use the fraction (central angle/360°), just applied to circumference or area respectively. For coordinate geometry, plug values carefully into the slope, midpoint, or distance formula, keeping track of which point is (x₁,y₁) and which is (x₂,y₂). For solids, match the shape to its correct volume or surface area formula, and remember that surface area means finding the total area of every face/surface of the solid.

Explanation

These formulas are consistent tools — once memorized, they turn a geometry problem into simple substitution and calculation. Arc length and sector area are really just circumference and area "scaled down" by whatever fraction of the full circle the central angle represents, which is why they share the same (angle/360°) fraction in their formulas.

1. Find the circumference of a circle with radius 7 (use π ≈ 22/7).
C = 2 × 22/7 × 7 = 44
2. A circle has radius 6 and a central angle of 60°. Find the arc length (use π ≈ 3.14).
(60/360) × 2π(6) = (1/6)(37.68) ≈ 6.28
3. Find the midpoint between (2,4) and (8,10).
((2+8)/2, (4+10)/2) = (5, 7)
4. Find the volume of a cylinder with radius 3 and height 10 (use π ≈ 3.14).
V = 3.14 × 9 × 10 = 282.6
5. Find the surface area of a rectangular prism with length 4, width 3, height 5.
SA = 2(4×3 + 4×5 + 3×5) = 2(12+20+15) = 2(47) = 94

Statistics, Probability, and Data

C4 — Statistics, Probability, and Data

▼
Meaning

This covers how to summarize a set of data (mean, median, mode, range), weighted averages, reading graphs and tables, calculating the likelihood of events, and organizing information using counting techniques and Venn diagrams.

Examples
  • Mean (average): sum of values ÷ number of values
  • Median: the middle value when data is arranged in order
  • Mode: the value that appears most often
  • Range: the difference between the highest and lowest values in a data set (max − min)
  • Weighted average: used when some values count more than others — e.g., if a final grade is 60% from exams (average 85) and 40% from projects (average 90), the weighted average = 0.60(85) + 0.40(90) = 87
  • Probability of a simple event: P = (favorable outcomes) / (total possible outcomes)
  • Counting principle: if there are 3 shirt choices and 2 pants choices, there are 3 × 2 = 6 possible outfit combinations (multiply the choices together)
  • Permutations vs combinations: a permutation counts arrangements where order matters (like arranging 1st, 2nd, 3rd place); a combination counts groupings where order doesn't matter (like choosing a committee of 3 people)
  • Venn diagrams: circles overlapping to show relationships between groups — the overlapping section represents elements that belong to both groups at once.
Application

For mean/median/mode/range questions, always arrange the data in order first. For weighted average problems, multiply each value by its corresponding weight (as a decimal or fraction) before adding them together — never just average the raw numbers without considering their weights. For counting principle problems, multiply the number of options at each stage together. For Venn diagram problems, carefully identify what the overlapping region and each separate region represents before doing any calculations, since misreading the diagram is the most common mistake.

Explanation

Statistics questions test whether you can organize and interpret data logically. Weighted averages exist because not all data points should count equally in real situations (like grades, where a final exam might matter more than a quiz), so recognizing when a simple average won't work correctly is an important skill. Venn diagrams give a visual way to organize overlapping categories, which makes set-logic word problems much easier to solve than trying to track everything with words alone.

1. Find the range of: 4, 8, 15, 2, 10.
15 − 2 = 13
2. A student's grade is 70% from exams (average 80) and 30% from homework (average 95). Find the weighted average.
0.70(80) + 0.30(95) = 56 + 28.5 = 84.5
3. A restaurant has 4 main dish choices and 3 drink choices. How many possible meal combinations are there?
4 × 3 = 12
4. Is choosing 3 books to bring on a trip (where order doesn't matter) a permutation or combination?
Combination — order doesn't matter, just which books are chosen.
5. In a class of 30 students, 18 play basketball, 12 play volleyball, and 5 play both. How many students play neither sport? (Venn diagram)
Students playing at least one sport = 18 + 12 − 5 = 25; students playing neither = 30 − 25 = 5

Common Word Problems

C5 — Common Word Problems

▼
Meaning

Word problems combine several math skills at once — you have to translate a real-world scenario into an equation and solve it. Common types include rate-time-distance, work problems, mixtures, age problems, ratio-sharing, percent applications (discounts/tax/profit), motion problems involving current or wind, and set/logic problems.

Examples
  • Distance formula: d = r × t
  • Work problems: if person A finishes a job in 4 hours and person B in 6 hours, their combined work rate is 1/4 + 1/6 per hour.
  • Age problems: often use "x years ago" or "in x years" to set up an equation.
  • Ratio sharing: if money is split in a 2:3 ratio, divide the total into 5 equal parts, then multiply by each ratio number.
  • Percent applications: discount = original price × discount rate (e.g., 20% off ₱500 = ₱500 × 0.20 = ₱100 off, so final price = ₱400); sales tax works the same way but adds to the price instead of subtracting; profit = selling price − cost price.
  • Motion with current/wind (upstream/downstream): if a boat's speed in still water is b, and the current's speed is c, the boat's actual speed is (b + c) going downstream (with the current) and (b − c) going upstream (against the current).
  • Set problems using Venn logic: similar to the Venn diagram statistics problems, but focused on translating word descriptions ("students who like both," "students who like neither") into a solvable equation.
Application

For any word problem, first identify what the unknown is and assign it a variable. Then translate the sentence piece by piece into a math equation using the relationships given. For percent application problems, always be clear on whether you're finding the discount amount, the final price after discount, or the original price before a discount was applied — these require slightly different setups. For motion-with-current problems, remember that the current helps you (adds to speed) when going downstream, and works against you (subtracts from speed) when going upstream.

Explanation

Word problems are testing whether you can bridge everyday language and math — the actual equations are often simple once set up correctly. The real skill is careful, step-by-step translation, not complicated math. Percent application and motion-with-current problems are especially common in real life (shopping, travel), which is exactly why they show up frequently on the exam.

Total parts = 5; each part = ₱200 → first gets ₱400, second gets ₱600

D0 — Scientific Thinking & Skills

▼
Meaning

Before testing specific science facts, the exam checks whether you understand how scientific investigation actually works — including the scientific method, variables, reading data from graphs and tables, converting between units, and evaluating an experiment's results and possible flaws.

Examples
  • Scientific method steps, in order: observation → hypothesis → experiment/procedure → conclusion
  • Independent variable: the one YOU deliberately change (e.g., amount of sunlight given to a plant)
  • Dependent variable: the one that responds/changes as a result (e.g., plant growth)
  • Control group: the group that does NOT receive the experimental treatment, used as a baseline.
  • Reading graphs and tables: always check the axis labels, units, and title first, then look at the overall trend (increasing, decreasing, staying flat) before reading specific values.
  • Unit conversion (metric): 1 kilometer = 1,000 meters; 1 meter = 100 centimeters; 1 liter = 1,000 milliliters; 1 kilogram = 1,000 grams — moving from a larger unit to a smaller one means multiplying, moving from smaller to larger means dividing.
  • Interpreting results and drawing conclusions: a conclusion should directly connect back to the original hypothesis, stating whether the data supported it or not.
  • Sources of error: factors that could make an experiment's results less accurate or reliable, such as a small sample size, uncontrolled variables, or measurement mistakes.
Application

When reading an experiment description, identify the independent variable, dependent variable, controlled variables, and control group. For graph and table questions, read the axis labels and units first, then identify the overall trend before answering specific detail questions. For unit conversion problems, identify whether you're converting from a larger unit to a smaller one (multiply) or smaller to larger (divide), and set up the conversion factor carefully. When asked to evaluate an experiment's conclusion, check whether the stated conclusion is actually supported by the data described, or whether it overreaches. When asked about sources of error, think about what wasn't controlled or what could have been measured inaccurately.

Explanation

Science, at its core, is about testing ideas fairly — by changing only one thing at a time and comparing against a baseline (control group), you can trust that a result was actually caused by what you changed. Reading graphs, converting units, and spotting sources of error are all practical skills that apply to virtually any experiment described on the exam, even ones covering unfamiliar topics.

1. In an experiment testing if fertilizer helps plants grow taller, what is the independent variable?
The amount/presence of fertilizer (the thing being changed on purpose).
2. Convert 3.5 kilometers to meters.
3.5 × 1,000 = 3,500 meters
3. A graph shows temperature rising steadily from 8 AM to 12 PM, then dropping sharply. What is the overall trend described?
Temperature increased steadily in the morning, then decreased sharply after noon.
4. An experiment tested only 3 plants and concluded that "all plants grow faster with fertilizer." What is a possible source of error?
Too small a sample size to reliably support such a broad conclusion.
5. Convert 2,500 milliliters to liters.
2,500 ÷ 1,000 = 2.5 liters

D1.1 — Biology — Cells and Life Processes

▼
Meaning

This topic covers the basic unit of life — the cell — including the two broad categories of cells, the jobs of different cell parts, how substances move in and out of cells, how cells produce and use energy, and how cells divide.

Examples
  • Cell theory: all living things are made of cells; cells are the basic unit of life.
  • Prokaryote vs eukaryote: prokaryotic cells (like bacteria) have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotic cells (like plant and animal cells) have a true nucleus and organelles enclosed in membranes.
  • Organelles: nucleus (control center), mitochondria (energy production), ribosome (protein-making), cell membrane (controls what enters/exits), chloroplast (photosynthesis, plants only)
  • Cell transport: diffusion (movement of particles from high to low concentration, no energy needed), osmosis (diffusion specifically of water across a membrane), active transport (movement that requires energy, often moving substances from low to high concentration, against the natural flow)
  • Photosynthesis vs cellular respiration: photosynthesis takes in carbon dioxide and water, and uses sunlight to produce glucose and oxygen (mainly in plants); cellular respiration takes in glucose and oxygen and releases energy, carbon dioxide, and water (happens in nearly all living cells, including plants and animals) — the inputs and outputs of one process are essentially the outputs and inputs of the other.
  • Cell division: mitosis produces two identical cells for growth and repair; meiosis produces four genetically different reproductive cells (sperm/egg) with half the usual number of chromosomes.
Application

For organelle questions, match each one to its specific job. For prokaryote vs eukaryote questions, the presence or absence of a nucleus is the key identifying feature. For cell transport questions, ask whether energy is required (active transport) or not (diffusion/osmosis), and whether the substance is moving with or against the natural concentration gradient. For photosynthesis vs. respiration questions, remember that they are essentially opposite processes — what goes in for one comes out of the other. For mitosis vs. meiosis, remember mitosis is for everyday growth/repair (identical cells), while meiosis is specifically for creating reproductive cells (different, half the chromosomes).

Explanation

Understanding the basic structure and function of cells provides the foundation for nearly every other biology topic, since all life processes ultimately happen at the cellular level. The relationship between photosynthesis and cellular respiration is one of the most elegant patterns in biology — recognizing that they're mirror-image processes makes both much easier to remember together rather than separately.

1. Does a bacterial cell have a nucleus? Is it prokaryotic or eukaryotic?
No nucleus — it is prokaryotic.
2. Which organelle is responsible for producing energy in a cell?
Mitochondria
3. Is diffusion an active or passive process (does it require energy)?
Passive — it does not require energy, since particles move naturally from high to low concentration.
4. What are the inputs of cellular respiration?
Glucose and oxygen
5. Which type of cell division produces four genetically different reproductive cells?
Meiosis

D1.2 — Biology — Genetics and Evolution

▼
Meaning

This topic covers how traits are passed down through genes, what DNA and chromosomes are, how mutations create variation, and how species change over time through natural selection, supported by evidence.

Examples
  • DNA, genes, and chromosomes: DNA is the molecule that carries genetic information; a gene is a specific segment of DNA that codes for a particular trait; a chromosome is a tightly packaged structure made of DNA, found in the nucleus.
  • Genotype vs phenotype: genotype = genetic makeup (e.g., Bb); phenotype = observable trait (e.g., brown eyes)
  • Punnett square: a grid used to predict the possible gene combinations of offspring
  • Mutations: changes in the DNA sequence; most are neutral or harmful, but some can be beneficial and provide an advantage.
  • Natural selection: organisms with traits better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more, passing on those traits.
  • Evidence for evolution: includes the fossil record (showing gradual change over time), similarities in anatomy between related species (like the same bone structure in a bat's wing and a human's arm), and DNA similarities between species.
Application

For genetics questions, remember that a dominant allele (capital letter) shows up in the phenotype even if only one copy is present, while a recessive trait (lowercase) only shows up if both copies are recessive. For Punnett square problems, cross each parent's alleles methodically. For mutation questions, remember that mutations are the actual source of new genetic variation, which natural selection then acts upon. For evolution evidence questions, connect each type of evidence (fossils, anatomy, DNA) to what it specifically demonstrates: fossils show change over time, similar anatomy suggests common ancestry, and DNA similarity directly measures how closely related two species are.

Explanation

Genetics questions are really about probability — a Punnett square just organizes all the possible combinations so you can count how many are dominant vs. recessive. Mutations and natural selection work together as a two-step process: mutations create new genetic variety (essentially by chance), and natural selection then determines which of those variations are more likely to survive and be passed on — this combination is what drives evolution over many generations.

1. What is the relationship between DNA, genes, and chromosomes?
DNA is the molecule carrying genetic information; a gene is a specific segment of DNA; a chromosome is a packaged structure made of DNA (containing many genes).
2. If a plant has genotype Bb (B = dominant brown flower, b = recessive white), what color are its flowers?
Brown (dominant allele B is expressed)
3. Cross two heterozygous parents (Bb × Bb). What fraction of offspring will be homozygous recessive (bb)?
1/4
4. Are most mutations helpful, harmful, or neutral?
Most are neutral or harmful; only some are beneficial.
5. What kind of evidence for evolution comes from comparing the bone structure of a bat's wing and a human's arm?
Anatomical similarity, suggesting a common ancestor.

D1.3 — Biology — Human Body Systems

▼
Meaning

This topic covers how the major systems in the human body work together to keep a person alive, healthy, and functioning — including digestion, circulation, breathing, control/response, defense against disease, waste removal, and reproduction.

Examples
  • Digestive system: breaks down food for energy and nutrients.
  • Circulatory system: transports blood, oxygen, and nutrients throughout the body.
  • Respiratory system: responsible for breathing — taking in oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide, mainly through the lungs.
  • Nervous system: controls responses and communication throughout the body via the brain and nerves.
  • Endocrine system: controls the body using hormones (chemical messengers) released into the blood, generally acting more slowly than the nervous system but with longer-lasting effects.
  • Immune system: defends the body against pathogens (harmful microorganisms like bacteria and viruses); involves white blood cells and antibodies.
  • Excretory system and homeostasis: removes waste products from the body (like the kidneys filtering blood to produce urine), helping maintain homeostasis — the body's stable internal environment.
  • Reproductive system: responsible for producing offspring; basic concepts include the roles of sperm and egg cells.
Application

When a question describes a body function, match it to the correct system. Distinguish the nervous system (fast, electrical signals, immediate responses) from the endocrine system (slower, hormone-based, longer-lasting effects) when a question describes the type of response. For immune system questions, connect the concept of pathogens (the invaders) with the body's defenses (white blood cells, antibodies) that fight them off. For excretory system questions, connect waste removal directly to the idea of homeostasis — keeping the body's internal conditions stable and balanced.

Explanation

The human body works as a set of connected systems, each with a specific job, similar to departments in an organization — no single system works in isolation. Homeostasis is a central, unifying idea across many of these systems, since the body is constantly working (through excretion, temperature regulation, and other means) to keep its internal environment stable despite outside changes.

1. Which body system is responsible for transporting oxygen throughout the body?
Circulatory system
2. Which system takes in oxygen and releases carbon dioxide?
Respiratory system
3. What is the key difference between how the nervous system and endocrine system send signals?
Nervous system uses fast electrical signals for immediate responses; endocrine system uses slower hormone signals for longer-lasting effects.
4. What is the role of white blood cells in the immune system?
They help defend the body against pathogens (harmful microorganisms).
5. What is homeostasis, and which system plays a major role in maintaining it through waste removal?
Homeostasis is the body's stable internal environment; the excretory system helps maintain it by removing waste products (e.g., kidneys filtering blood).

D1.4 — Biology — Ecology

▼
Meaning

This topic covers how living things interact with each other and their environment, including the flow of energy through an ecosystem, the recycling of matter through natural cycles, and the impact humans have on the natural world.

Examples
  • Food chain/web: shows how energy flows from producers (plants) to consumers (animals) to decomposers.
  • Trophic levels: the different feeding positions in a food chain (producers, primary consumers, secondary consumers, etc.); energy decreases significantly at each higher level.
  • Energy pyramid: a visual model showing that energy decreases as you move up each trophic level — this is why there are far fewer top predators than plants in an ecosystem.
  • Biogeochemical cycles: water cycle (evaporation, condensation, precipitation), carbon cycle (movement of carbon between the atmosphere, living things, and the ocean/soil), and nitrogen cycle (movement of nitrogen between the atmosphere, soil, and living things) — all describe how matter is continuously recycled through an ecosystem.
  • Population vs community vs ecosystem: a population is all the members of one species in an area; a community is all the different populations (species) living together in an area; an ecosystem includes the community plus the non-living environment (water, soil, climate).
  • Biodiversity and conservation: biodiversity refers to the variety of living things in an ecosystem; conservation refers to efforts to protect and preserve that variety.
  • Human impacts: pollution, habitat loss, and climate change are major ways human activity affects ecosystems and biodiversity.
Application

For food chain/energy pyramid questions, remember that energy always flows in one direction (from producers upward) and decreases significantly at each level. For cycle questions (water, carbon, nitrogen), track how the specific substance moves between the atmosphere, living things, and the physical environment. For population/community/ecosystem questions, remember the size relationship: population is the smallest and most specific (one species), community is broader (multiple species), and ecosystem is broadest (living things plus non-living environment).

Explanation

Ecology ties together how individual organisms depend on each other and their surroundings — removing or disrupting one part of the system, such as a key species or a natural cycle, can affect the balance of the entire community. Recognizing the size relationship between population, community, and ecosystem helps you correctly answer questions that test the specific scope of each term.

1. In a food chain: grass → rabbit → fox, which organism is the producer?
Grass (it makes its own food through photosynthesis)
2. What happens to the amount of available energy as you move up each trophic level in an energy pyramid?
It decreases significantly at each higher level.
3. Which natural cycle describes the movement of water through evaporation, condensation, and precipitation?
The water cycle
4. What is the difference between a population and a community?
A population is all members of one species in an area; a community includes all the different species (populations) living together in that area.
5. Name two major human impacts on ecosystems mentioned in this topic.
Pollution and habitat loss (or climate change)

D2.1 — Chemistry — Matter, Atomic Structure, and the Periodic Table

▼
Meaning

This topic covers the different states matter can exist in, the basic structure of an atom, and how the periodic table organizes elements based on their properties.

Examples
  • States of matter: solid (fixed shape and volume), liquid (fixed volume, takes the shape of its container), gas (no fixed shape or volume, fills its container); phase changes include melting (solid→liquid), freezing (liquid→solid), evaporation (liquid→gas), and condensation (gas→liquid).
  • Atomic structure: an atom is made of protons (positive charge, in the nucleus), neutrons (no charge, in the nucleus), and electrons (negative charge, orbiting the nucleus). The number of protons determines what element it is.
  • Isotopes: atoms of the same element with a different number of neutrons (same protons, different mass).
  • Periodic table organization: elements are arranged by increasing atomic number; groups (columns) share similar chemical properties; periods (rows) show a trend across increasing atomic number; the table is divided into metals, nonmetals, and metalloids.
  • Element vs compound vs mixture: an element is one type of atom; a compound is two or more elements chemically combined; a mixture is a physical combination that isn't chemically bonded.
  • Ions and valence: an ion is an atom that has gained or lost electrons, giving it a charge; valence electrons (in the outermost shell) determine how an atom will bond with others.
Application

For states of matter questions, connect each phase change to whether energy is being added (melting, evaporation) or removed (freezing, condensation). For atomic structure questions, remember: protons and neutrons are in the nucleus (protons = positive, neutrons = neutral), while electrons orbit around it (negative). For periodic table questions, remember elements in the same group (column) share similar properties because they have the same number of valence electrons. For ion questions, connect the gain or loss of electrons to whether the resulting charge is negative (gained electrons) or positive (lost electrons).

Explanation

Understanding atomic structure is the foundation for understanding how and why atoms bond together, since bonding is fundamentally about how atoms gain, lose, or share their outermost (valence) electrons to become more stable. The periodic table's organization isn't random — it directly reflects these underlying atomic properties, which is why elements in the same column behave similarly.

1. What phase change occurs when a liquid turns into a gas?
Evaporation
2. What particle in an atom has a positive charge, and where is it located?
Proton, located in the nucleus
3. Two atoms of the same element have different numbers of neutrons. What are they called?
Isotopes
4. Elements in the same column (group) of the periodic table tend to have similar what?
Chemical properties (because they have the same number of valence electrons)
5. If an atom loses an electron, does it become positively or negatively charged?
Positively charged (it becomes a positive ion, since it now has more protons than electrons)

D2.2 — Chemistry — Bonding and Reactions

▼
Meaning

This topic covers how atoms bond together to form compounds, the different types of chemical reactions, and the rule that governs how matter behaves during a reaction.

Examples
  • Ionic vs covalent bonding: ionic bonds transfer electrons between atoms (typically metal + nonmetal); covalent bonds share electrons (typically nonmetal + nonmetal).
  • Types of reactions: synthesis (two or more substances combine into one, A + B → AB), decomposition (one substance breaks into two or more, AB → A + B), single replacement (one element replaces another in a compound, A + BC → AC + B), double replacement (parts of two compounds switch places, AB + CD → AD + CB)
  • Balancing equations: the number of atoms of each element must be equal on both sides of a chemical equation (conservation of mass).
  • Conservation of mass: matter is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction — the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.
Application

For bonding questions, check whether you're dealing with a metal + nonmetal (ionic) or two nonmetals (covalent). For reaction type questions, count how many substances start on the left side versus how many end up on the right side — this pattern usually reveals whether it's a synthesis, decomposition, or replacement reaction. For balancing equations, count atoms of each element on both sides and adjust coefficients (not subscripts) until they match.

Explanation

Recognizing bonding and reaction patterns turns what looks like a complicated chemistry problem into pattern recognition — metals bond with nonmetals by transferring electrons, two nonmetals share electrons, and reactions can usually be sorted into one of just a few basic types based on how many substances go in versus come out. Balancing equations reflects the basic law that matter can't be created or destroyed in an ordinary reaction.

1. Would you expect table salt (NaCl, a metal + nonmetal) to have ionic or covalent bonding?
Ionic bonding
2. Classify this reaction: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
Synthesis (two substances combine into one)
3. Classify this reaction: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂
Decomposition (one substance breaks into two)
4. Balance this equation: N₂ + H₂ → NH₃
N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃
5. According to the law of conservation of mass, if 10g of reactants are used in a reaction, how many grams of products should form?
10g (mass is conserved — neither created nor destroyed)

D2.3 — Chemistry — Acids, Bases, and Solutions

▼
Meaning

This topic covers how to identify acids and bases, what happens when they react, how indicators are used to test pH, how solutions work, and basic gas law relationships.

Examples
  • pH scale: below 7 = acidic, 7 = neutral, above 7 = basic (alkaline)
  • Indicators: substances that change color depending on whether a solution is acidic or basic (e.g., litmus paper turns red in acid and blue in base) — used to quickly test a solution's approximate pH.
  • Neutralization: acid + base → salt + water
  • Solute vs solvent: solute is the substance being dissolved; solvent is what does the dissolving
  • Concentration: how much solute is dissolved in a given amount of solvent
  • Solubility factors: temperature usually affects how much solute can dissolve in a solvent (most solids dissolve faster/more in warmer liquids)
  • Simple gas laws (conceptual): as the volume of a gas decreases, its pressure increases (if temperature stays the same); as temperature increases, gas particles move faster and pressure/volume tend to increase as well — these are basic pressure-volume-temperature relationships.
Application

For pH questions, remember lower numbers mean more acidic, higher numbers mean more basic. For indicator questions, connect the specific color change described to whether the solution is acidic or basic. For neutralization questions, recognize that mixing an acid and a base produces a neutral salt and water. For gas law questions, remember the general relationship: decreasing volume increases pressure (and vice versa), assuming temperature is constant.

Explanation

The pH scale and indicators give us practical, testable ways to determine how acidic or basic a substance is without needing complex lab equipment. Gas law relationships describe consistent, predictable patterns in how gas particles behave under different conditions, which is why they can be summarized as simple, general rules even though the full science behind them is more complex.

1. Is a substance with pH 3 acidic, neutral, or basic?
Acidic (below 7)
2. If litmus paper turns blue when dipped in a solution, is the solution acidic or basic?
Basic
3. What are the products of an acid-base neutralization reaction?
Salt and water
4. Does most solid solute dissolve better in warm or cold solvent?
Warm (higher temperature generally increases solubility of solids)
5. If you decrease the volume of a gas while keeping temperature constant, what happens to its pressure?
It increases

D3.1 — Physics — Motion and Forces

▼
Meaning

This topic covers how objects move, how to read motion from graphs, and the forces (including friction) that affect movement, along with basic concepts of momentum.

Examples
  • Speed vs velocity: speed is just how fast (a number); velocity includes direction.
  • Distance-time graph: a steeper line means faster speed; a flat (horizontal) line means the object is not moving.
  • Velocity-time graph: the slope represents acceleration; a flat line means constant velocity (no acceleration).
  • Newton's First Law (inertia): an object stays at rest or in motion unless acted on by a force.
  • Newton's Second Law: F = ma
  • Friction: a force that opposes motion between two surfaces in contact; more friction means more resistance to movement.
  • Normal force: the support force a surface exerts on an object resting on it, perpendicular to the surface.
  • Momentum: a measure of an object's motion, calculated as mass × velocity (p = mv); a heavier or faster-moving object has more momentum.
  • Impulse: a change in momentum, related to the force applied and how long it's applied.
Application

For distance-time and velocity-time graph questions, focus on the slope of the line — a steeper slope means a faster rate of change. For force questions, apply F = ma directly. For friction questions, remember it always acts opposite to the direction of motion. For momentum questions, remember that both mass and velocity matter — a small fast object and a large slow object can have the same momentum.

Explanation

Reading motion graphs is really about interpreting slope as a rate — distance-time graphs show speed through their slope, and velocity-time graphs show acceleration the same way. Newton's laws and the concept of friction describe consistent, predictable rules about how forces affect motion, which is why memorizing just a few key relationships (F=ma, momentum = mv) covers most of what's tested here.

1. On a distance-time graph, what does a steeper line indicate?
A faster speed
2. On a velocity-time graph, what does a flat (horizontal) line indicate?
Constant velocity (no acceleration)
3. A 10 kg object accelerates at 2 m/s². What force is applied?
F = 10 × 2 = 20 N
4. Which direction does friction act, relative to an object's motion?
Opposite to the direction of motion
5. Calculate the momentum of a 5 kg object moving at 4 m/s.
p = mv = 5 × 4 = 20 kg·m/s

D3.2 — Physics — Energy, Work, Power, and Simple Machines

▼
Meaning

This topic covers the different forms of energy, how work and power are calculated, and how simple machines make work easier.

Examples
  • Potential vs kinetic energy: potential = stored energy (position-based); kinetic = energy of motion
  • Conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another.
  • Work: in physics, work = force × distance (W = Fd); work is only done when a force actually causes movement in the direction of the force.
  • Power: the rate at which work is done, power = work ÷ time (P = W/t)
  • Simple machines: tools that make work easier by changing the amount of force needed or the direction of the force — levers (a rigid bar that pivots on a fixed point), pulleys (a wheel with a rope, used to lift objects), inclined planes, and others all reduce the effort needed, though the total work stays roughly the same.
Application

For energy questions, track how energy changes form (e.g., potential converts to kinetic as an object falls) rather than assuming it disappears. For work/power questions, apply the formulas directly: W = Fd and P = W/t. For simple machine questions, remember that these tools trade off force for distance — you might apply less force, but you often have to apply it over a longer distance, so the total work done stays about the same (ignoring friction).

Explanation

Energy conservation is one of the most important ideas in physics — energy just transforms from one type to another, it never truly vanishes. Simple machines are a practical application of this same principle: they don't reduce the total work needed, they just let you trade force for distance (or vice versa), which is often more convenient in real life, like using a ramp instead of lifting something straight up.

1. A ball is held at the top of a hill (not moving). What type of energy does it mainly have?
Potential energy
2. Calculate the work done when a force of 20 N moves an object 5 meters.
W = 20 × 5 = 100 J (joules)
3. If 100 J of work is done in 5 seconds, what is the power?
P = 100/5 = 20 W (watts)
4. What is the main benefit of using a lever or pulley (a simple machine)?
It reduces the amount of force needed to do the same amount of work (though usually over a longer distance).
5. As a ball rolls down a hill, what happens to its potential and kinetic energy?
Potential energy decreases while kinetic energy increases.

D3.3 — Physics — Waves, Light, Sound, Electricity, and Magnetism

▼
Meaning

This topic covers the basic properties of waves (light and sound), how mirrors and lenses affect light, and the fundamentals of electric circuits and magnetism.

Examples
  • Wave parts: wavelength, frequency, amplitude
  • Wave speed relationship: wave speed = wavelength × frequency (v = fλ) — if frequency increases while speed stays constant, wavelength must decrease.
  • Sound: pitch relates to frequency; loudness relates to amplitude.
  • Light: reflection (bouncing off a surface) vs refraction (bending when passing through a different medium)
  • Mirrors and lenses (basic concept): a mirror reflects light to form an image; a lens bends (refracts) light to form an image — convex lenses/mirrors curve outward, concave ones curve inward, and each affects light differently.
  • Charge, current, voltage, resistance (basic meanings): charge is a basic property of matter (positive or negative); current is the flow of electric charge; voltage is the "push" that drives current through a circuit; resistance opposes the flow of current.
  • Ohm's Law: V = IR
  • Series vs parallel circuits: series = one shared path; parallel = multiple separate paths.
  • Magnets: have two poles (north and south); like poles repel, opposite poles attract; magnetic fields surround a magnet; an electromagnet is created when electric current flows through a coiled wire, generating a magnetic field.
Application

For wave questions, use v = fλ to relate speed, frequency, and wavelength, and connect frequency to pitch/color. For light questions, distinguish reflection (bouncing) from refraction (bending through a new medium), and connect convex/concave shapes to how they focus or spread light. For circuit questions, apply Ohm's Law directly. For magnetism questions, remember the basic rule: like poles repel, opposite poles attract, and that electric current can generate a magnetic field (the basis of an electromagnet).

Explanation

Waves, light, and electricity all follow consistent, describable rules, which is why a relatively small set of formulas and concepts (wave speed formula, Ohm's Law, the basic rules of magnetism) can cover a wide range of exam questions. Electromagnets are a particularly useful concept to understand well, since they demonstrate the close relationship between electricity and magnetism — one can create the other.

1. If a wave has a frequency of 5 Hz and a wavelength of 2 m, what is its speed?
v = fλ = 5 × 2 = 10 m/s
2. If a sound wave has a higher frequency, is its pitch higher or lower?
Higher
3. What is the difference between reflection and refraction of light?
Reflection is light bouncing off a surface; refraction is light bending as it passes through a different medium.
4. A circuit has voltage 12V and resistance 4Ω. What is the current?
I = V/R = 12/4 = 3 A
5. Do like magnetic poles attract or repel each other?
Repel

D4 — Earth and Space Science

▼
Meaning

This topic covers the internal structure of the Earth, rocks and soil, weather and the water cycle, and basic astronomy including the solar system, seasons, moon phases, eclipses, and natural hazards.

Examples
  • Earth's layers: crust (outer, thinnest), mantle (middle, thickest), core (inner, hottest and densest)
  • Plate tectonics: the Earth's crust is broken into plates that move slowly, causing earthquakes and volcanoes at their boundaries.
  • Rocks and the rock cycle: igneous rocks form from cooled magma/lava, sedimentary rocks form from compressed layers of sediment, and metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are changed by heat and pressure; the rock cycle describes how rocks can transform between these three types over time. Soil forms from weathered rock combined with organic material.
  • Water cycle: evaporation (water turns to vapor), condensation (vapor forms clouds), precipitation (rain/snow falls), collection (water gathers in bodies of water) — a continuous cycle.
  • Weather vs climate: weather is short-term, climate is the long-term pattern.
  • Solar system: the sun is at the center, with planets orbiting it; Earth is the third planet from the sun.
  • Seasons: caused by the tilt of the Earth's axis, not by distance from the sun.
  • Moon phases: caused by the changing angle at which we see the sunlit part of the moon.
  • Eclipses: a solar eclipse happens when the moon passes between the Earth and sun, blocking sunlight; a lunar eclipse happens when the Earth passes between the sun and moon, casting a shadow on the moon.
  • Natural hazards: typhoons, floods, and landslides are common natural hazards, especially relevant in the Philippines; preparedness involves having emergency plans and supplies ready in advance.
Application

For Earth layer questions, remember the order: crust → mantle → core. For rock cycle questions, connect each rock type to how it forms (cooling for igneous, compression for sedimentary, heat/pressure for metamorphic). For water cycle questions, know the four main stages in order. For astronomy questions, always attribute seasons to Earth's tilt (not distance from the sun), and distinguish solar eclipses (moon blocks the sun) from lunar eclipses (Earth's shadow falls on the moon).

Explanation

Earth science ties together how the planet's physical structure and natural cycles (rock cycle, water cycle) shape the environment we live in, while astronomy questions often test common misconceptions, like the true cause of seasons or the difference between the two types of eclipses. Understanding the "why" behind each phenomenon makes it easier to answer variations of the same concept, and natural hazard awareness is especially practical and relevant for students in the Philippines.

2. Which type of rock forms from cooled magma or lava?

3. List the four stages of the water cycle in order.

4. What actually causes the seasons on Earth?

5. What is the difference between a solar eclipse and a lunar eclipse?
A solar eclipse is when the moon blocks sunlight from reaching Earth (moon between Earth and sun); a lunar eclipse is when Earth's shadow falls on the moon (Earth between sun and moon).

Reasoning Skills

E1 — Reasoning Skills

▼
Meaning

This covers basic logical thinking skills — recognizing patterns in a sequence, understanding basic logic operators (AND, OR, NOT), understanding how "if-then" statements work and what they actually guarantee, and evaluating whether the conclusion of an argument truly and necessarily follows from its starting premises. These are less about memorizing facts and more about training yourself to reason carefully, step by step.

Examples
  • Number pattern: 2, 4, 8, 16, ___ (each term doubles the one before it) → the next term is 32
  • Basic logic operators:
  • AND: a statement using "and" is only true if both parts are true. "It is raining AND it is cold" is only true if both conditions hold at the same time.
  • OR: a statement using "or" is true if at least one part is true (in everyday logic, this usually includes the case where both are true). "It is raining OR it is cold" is true if either one (or both) is happening.
  • NOT: reverses the truth value of a statement. "It is NOT raining" is true exactly when "it is raining" is false.
  • Combining operators: "It is raining AND NOT cold" is only true if it's raining, and it is also not cold.
  • If-then statement: "If it rains, then the ground gets wet." The contrapositive of this statement ("If the ground is not wet, then it did not rain") is also guaranteed to be true. However, the converse ("If the ground is wet, then it rained") is not necessarily true, since someone could have watered the lawn with a hose instead.
  • Syllogism: "All dogs are animals. Rex is a dog. Therefore, Rex is an animal." (this is valid — the conclusion follows logically and necessarily from the two premises given)
  • Invalid syllogism: "All dogs are animals. Rex is an animal. Therefore, Rex is a dog." (this is invalid — Rex could just as easily be a cat, a bird, or any other kind of animal, not necessarily a dog)
  • Strengthen/weaken an argument: this means identifying what new piece of evidence, if added, would make a given argument either more convincing or less convincing than it currently is.
Application

For pattern questions, carefully check the relationship between each pair of consecutive terms before you commit to an answer. For logic operator questions, carefully test each part of the statement separately: for AND, check that both parts are true; for OR, check that at least one part is true; for NOT, simply flip the truth value of what follows. For if-then logic questions, remember clearly that only the contrapositive of a true statement is guaranteed to also be true — the converse and the inverse are not automatically true, even though they might sound very similar and tempting at first glance. For syllogism questions, carefully check whether the stated conclusion is truly the only possible outcome given the premises.

Explanation

Logic questions are specifically designed to test whether you can reason through a problem carefully and systematically, rather than simply jumping to whichever conclusion "feels" intuitively correct at first glance. Basic logic operators (AND, OR, NOT) form the building blocks behind more complex reasoning questions, including if-then statements and syllogisms, so getting comfortable with how they combine is genuinely useful groundwork. Many of the wrong answer choices in this section are deliberately designed to look correct on a quick read, which is exactly why slowing down to actually check the underlying logical structure, rather than relying on gut instinct, is the key skill this topic is really testing.

1. What comes next in the pattern? 3, 6, 12, 24, ___
48 (each term doubles)
2. Is the statement "2 + 2 = 4 AND 3 + 3 = 5" true or false?
False — while the first part is true, the second part is false, and AND requires both parts to be true.
3. Is the statement "2 + 2 = 4 OR 3 + 3 = 5" true or false?
True — OR only requires at least one part to be true, and the first part is true.
4. Given "If it rains, then the ground gets wet," which is guaranteed true: the converse or the contrapositive?
The contrapositive ("If the ground is not wet, then it did not rain")
5. Is this syllogism valid? "All cats are mammals. All mammals are animals. Therefore, all cats are animals."
Valid — the conclusion logically follows from both premises.
6. Is this syllogism valid? "Some fruits are red. Apples are fruits. Therefore, apples are red."
Invalid — "some fruits are red" doesn't mean all fruits (including apples) are red.
7. What kind of evidence would weaken this argument: "Ice cream sales and drowning incidents both rise in summer, so ice cream causes drowning"?
Evidence showing a third factor (like hot weather driving both more swimming and more ice cream sales) would weaken the claim that one causes the other.

Test-Taking Checklist

E2 — Test-Taking Checklist

▼
Meaning

Beyond just knowing the content, how you manage your time and approach each individual question during the actual exam has a genuinely significant effect on your final score. This topic is really a set of practical habits worth deliberately building well before test day arrives, so they feel natural and automatic once you're actually sitting for the exam under time pressure.

Checklist to build as habits
  • ☐ Underline EXCEPT/NOT/LEAST in a question before answering, so you don't accidentally answer it as if it were a normal, straightforward question.
  • ☐ Use elimination actively — cross out any answer options you're confident are wrong, even if you're not yet sure which specific option is correct, since this alone significantly boosts your odds of guessing correctly if needed.
  • ☐ Budget your time deliberately — if a particular question is taking far too long, skip it for now and come back to it later, rather than letting yourself get stuck and losing time you'll need for other questions.
  • ☐ In math and science questions, estimate your expected answer first, then check afterward if your final calculated answer is reasonably close to that estimate — this simple habit catches many careless errors before you submit.
  • ☐ Be extra cautious with answer choices containing extreme words like "always" or "never" — these are often incorrect unless the passage or formula clearly and explicitly supports that strong of a claim.
  • ☐ Practice a full mock exam on a weekly basis, and spend noticeably more time reviewing and understanding your mistakes afterward than simply repeating questions you already know how to answer correctly.
  • ☐ Prepare your test-day essentials well in advance: get proper sleep the night before, bring snacks and water, bring extra pencils, and plan out a calm, unhurried morning routine ahead of time.
Application

Build these habits into your regular practice sessions starting now, rather than waiting until test day itself — for example, make it an automatic habit to always underline EXCEPT/NOT questions whenever you're doing practice sets, so that this reflex becomes second nature well before the actual exam. When reviewing your mock exam results, spend real, focused time genuinely understanding why you got each particular question wrong, since this kind of targeted reflection is considerably more valuable in the long run than simply noting down the correct answer and moving on.

Explanation

A significant part of overall test performance actually comes down to consistent habits and strategy, not just raw content knowledge — for instance, the simple habit of skipping a difficult question and returning to it later can end up saving you several easy points elsewhere on the exam that you might otherwise have missed due to running out of time. These habits work best, and feel most natural, when they've been practiced repeatedly during preparation well before the actual test date, so that they can be executed almost automatically even while you're under real time pressure and some degree of stress.

1. You're stuck on a hard math question and have spent 3 minutes without progress. What should you do?
Skip it, mark it, and come back later if time allows — don't let one question eat into time for others.
2. You see an answer choice that says "This is always true in every case." What should you do?
Be cautious — extreme words like "always" or "never" are often wrong unless clearly supported by the passage/formula.
3. You've finished a math problem and got an answer of 450 for a question asking about a person's age. What should you do?
Recheck your work — this answer doesn't make sense for a person's age (estimation/reasonableness check).
4. During review of a mock exam, should you spend more time on questions you got right or wrong?
Questions you got wrong — understanding your mistakes is more valuable than repeating what you already know.
5. What's a good test-day habit the night before the exam?
Get enough sleep, prepare your materials (pencils, snacks, water), and avoid last-minute cramming that could cause stress.
